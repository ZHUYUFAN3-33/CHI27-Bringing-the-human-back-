# Results (draft)

> **TRIAL STORYLINE — draft text.** Organised in the order of the trial storyline (`story.md`). Evidence levels are tagged in brackets and must survive editing: [confirmatory], [equivalence], [secondary], [exploratory]. All numbers come from `numbers_sheet.md` / `tables.md`; the primary sample is *n* = 272 unless stated.

## 4.1 Preliminary checks

*Credibility of the descriptions.* Participants believed the description they had been given (BEL1: *M* = 5.20, *SD* = 1.52 on the 7-point scale; 76% rated 5 or higher, 17% rated 3 or lower), and belief did not differ between conditions (omnibus *p* = .451; human-involved versus AI-only Δ = −0.28, *p* = .302). The three descriptions were therefore equally credible.

*Evidence that the control-source text was read.* Among participants told that a human operated OriHime, those also told that AI assisted the operator rated the operator as less in control of what OriHime said and did (human 6.14 vs human+AI 5.73; Δ = 0.40 [0.14, 0.66], *d* = .40, *p* = .002) [confirmatory]. Disclosing a disability did not change perceived control (Δ = −0.16, *p* = .245).

*Structure of the ratings.* Between-participant differences accounted for most of the variance in every clip-level rating (intraclass correlations .40–.78), and the three clips differed strongly: relative to the relational clip, the advice clip raised the OriHime evaluation by 0.36 [0.26, 0.47] and the collaboration clip by 0.20 [0.09, 0.30] scale points (both *p* < .001); genuineness and controller warmth also rose across presentation positions (*p* < .001). Omnibus likelihood-ratio tests for the seven-level condition factor were significant for genuineness (*p* = .004), marginal for warmth (*p* = .075), and non-significant for the OriHime evaluation (*p* = .326) and competence (*p* = .572); the pre-specified contrasts below are the planned decomposition of this factor.

## 4.2 A human behind the robot makes the interaction feel genuine and the controller warm [confirmatory, C1]

Compared with the six human-involved conditions, the AI-only description lowered perceived genuineness by 0.81 scale points (95% CI 0.35–1.27; *d* = .62 [.26, .97]; *p* < .001, Holm-adjusted *p* = .002; Table 2, Figure 2). This effect was robust: it held in all 300 completers, without straightliners, with adjustment for prior attitudes and age, under Welch's *t* (*p* = .017), the Mann–Whitney test (*p* = .023), a participant cluster bootstrap (*p* = .011) and an ordinal model (OR = 2.23 [1.17, 4.23], *p* = .014; Table 3). Every one of the six human-involved cells scored higher than the AI-only cell, and three of these pairwise differences (H1, H3 and HA1 versus A) survived Holm correction within the 21-comparison family.

The controller was also rated as less warm under AI-only control (Δ = 0.50 [0.16, 0.84]; *d* = .52 [.17, .87]; *p* = .004, Holm *p* = .011). This effect held under the bootstrap (*p* = .031), the ordinal model (OR = 2.35, *p* = .030) and covariate adjustment (*p* < .001), but fell just short of significance under Welch's *t* (*p* = .052) and the Mann–Whitney test (*p* = .092); because between-participant variance was 1.3–1.5 times larger in the AI-only cell than in the human cells (Levene *p* ≤ .014), we regard it as suggestive rather than robust.

The overall OriHime evaluation was lower under AI-only control (Δ = 0.40 [0.06, 0.73]; *d* = .42 [.07, .77]; *p* = .020, Holm *p* = .040), again significant in the model-based analyses but not in the variance-robust ones (Welch *p* = .069, Mann–Whitney *p* = .063, bootstrap *p* = .055). Its components separate the effect: usefulness was lower under AI-only control (Δ = 0.38 [0.08, 0.68], *p* = .013; bootstrap *p* = .036; ordinal OR = 1.90, *p* = .031), willingness to take part was lower with less precision (Δ = 0.56 [0.03, 1.09], *p* = .039; bootstrap *p* = .085), whereas trustworthiness did not differ detectably (Δ = 0.25 [−0.05, 0.54], *p* = .099; bootstrap *p* = .145). Perceived competence of the controller did not differ (Δ = 0.22 [−0.07, 0.52]; *d* = .27; *p* = .135).

The pattern across the full pairwise map was the same for all four primary outcomes (Figure 1; Supplementary Table S1): of the 84 pairwise comparisons, the 10 with unadjusted *p* < .05 all contrasted a human-involved cell with the AI-only cell, and none of the 60 comparisons among the six human-involved cells reached *p* < .05. At the level of control source, both the human and the human+AI conditions exceeded the AI-only condition on genuineness (*d* = .64 and .53; *p* < .001 and .003) and warmth (*d* = .46 and .51; *p* = .006 and .007). Clip did not moderate the human-versus-AI contrast (likelihood-ratio tests, *p* ≥ .13 for the primary outcomes; Figure 3); within each clip the genuineness difference was significant (*p* ≤ .047).

## 4.3 What does not change: AI assistance, and who the operator is [equivalence, C2–C4]

*Human versus human with AI assistance.* Participants told that AI assisted the operator gave the same ratings as those told that the operator worked alone: OriHime evaluation *d* = −.08 [−.34, .17] (*p* = .539), genuineness *d* = .12 (*p* = .377), warmth *d* = .02 (*p* = .912), competence *d* = −.17 (*p* = .209), performance expectancy *d* = −.17 (*p* = .209), hedonic motivation *d* = −.04 (*p* = .747). Equivalence tests with bounds of ±0.35 *SD* rejected differences of that size for the OriHime evaluation (*p* = .020), genuineness (*p* = .047), warmth (*p* = .006) and hedonic motivation (*p* = .009), and with bounds of ±0.50 *SD* for every outcome (*p* ≤ .006; Table 4). Human and human+AI operation were thus statistically equivalent within half a standard deviation on all perception measures, and within a third of a standard deviation on most, even though the AI-assistance text had measurably reduced perceived operator control (4.1).

*Disclosure of the operator's disability.* Pooling the two disability descriptions against the no-mention cells, disclosure produced small negative point estimates on every outcome — OriHime evaluation *d* = −.10 (*p* = .491), genuineness *d* = −.24 [−.51, .03] (*p* = .112), warmth *d* = −.25 [−.52, .03] (*p* = .114), competence *d* = −.14 (*p* = .342), operator in control *d* = −.16 (*p* = .245), performance expectancy *d* = −.13 (*p* = .361), hedonic motivation *d* = −.26 (*p* = .076) — none significant. The two nearest-to-significant differences shrank after adjustment for prior attitudes and age (genuineness Δ = −0.10, *p* = .570; warmth Δ = −0.18, *p* = .166), consistent with the no-mention cells having, by chance, somewhat older and more AI-positive participants. Equivalence tests bounded the disclosure effect within ±0.35 *SD* for the OriHime evaluation (*p* = .040) and within ±0.50 *SD* for genuineness, warmth, competence, performance expectancy and hedonic motivation (*p* ≤ .032; Table 4). The two types of disability did not differ from each other on any outcome (|*d*| ≤ .17; equivalence within ±0.35 *SD* for all outcomes except genuineness), which supports pooling them. Disclosure did not interact with control source (likelihood-ratio *p* ≥ .18) or with clip (*p* ≥ .068), and did not depend on participants' contact with people with disabilities (*p* ≥ .36) or on their stereotype ratings of people with disabilities (*p* ≥ .27; 20 moderator tests, none significant) [exploratory]. Disclosure also left attribution unchanged (below).

## 4.4 AI involvement moves responsibility and credit away from the operator [confirmatory, RQ4]

When a human operated OriHime without AI, participants ranked the operator first for responsibility in 72% of clips and first for credit in 71%. Adding AI assistance halved the odds of ranking the operator first (responsibility: 55%, OR = 0.47 [0.31, 0.73], *p* = .001; credit: 51%, OR = 0.42 [0.27, 0.64], *p* < .001; Table 5). The lost share went to the AI: Plackett–Luce worths (Table 6, Figure 4) fell from .68 [.62, .75] for the operator working alone to .45 [.38, .53] with AI assistance, while the AI received .28 [.25, .32] and the shares of OriHime's provider (.19 → .18) and of the person in the video (.13 → .09) did not rise. When the operator was ranked first in the human+AI condition, the AI was ranked second in 64–67% of rankings. With no human present, the AI was ranked first in 57% (responsibility) and 60% (credit) of clips (OR versus human+AI ≈ 7.8, *p* < .001), with a worth of .55 [.43, .66]; the remainder went mostly to OriHime's provider (.30). Disclosing the operator's disability did not change the odds of the operator being ranked first (responsibility OR = 0.89 [0.57, 1.40], *p* = .624; credit OR = 0.80 [0.51, 1.25], *p* = .322).

In the human+AI condition, the operator was slightly more often ranked first for responsibility than for credit (55.4% vs 50.6% of clips per participant, +4.8 points; Wilcoxon *p* = .024; GEE OR = 1.21, *p* = .032; no interaction with clip, *p* = .89) [secondary]. No such asymmetry appeared for the operator working alone (+1.1 points, *p* = .536) or for the AI in the AI-only condition (−2.8 points, *p* = .433).

## 4.5 General acceptance did not depend on condition [secondary]

Performance expectancy (omnibus *p* = .610) and hedonic motivation (*p* = .395) did not differ across the seven conditions, and no pre-specified contrast reached significance (Table 2). A marginal difference in hedonic motivation between the disclosure and no-mention cells (Δ = −0.40 [−0.84, 0.04], *p* = .076) was traced to a single cell (HA1, *M* = 5.61 versus 4.85–5.16 in the other five human cells) and disappeared after covariate adjustment (Δ = −0.21, *p* = .263); we do not interpret it.

## 4.6 Exploratory analyses [exploratory; 113 tests, all listed in Supplementary Table S2]

*Who pays the AI-only penalty.* Participants' prior attitude to AI (GAAIS positive subscale, measured before the disclosure) moderated the human-versus-AI contrast. Ratings rose more steeply with positive attitude in the AI-only condition than in the human-involved conditions (slopes per scale point .50 vs .28 for the OriHime evaluation, interaction *p* = .019; .38 vs .10 for competence, *p* = .003; .48 vs .23 for genuineness, *p* = .083; .28 vs .10 for warmth, *p* = .093; Figure 5). Among participants one *SD* below the mean attitude, the AI-only penalty was 0.44 (OriHime evaluation, *p* = .014), 0.91 (genuineness, *p* < .001), 0.62 (warmth, *p* = .003) and 0.42 scale points (competence, *p* = .015); among participants one *SD* above the mean, no difference was significant (−0.20 to +0.20; *p* ≥ .10). The human+AI slope (.30) matched the human slope (.26): attitude-dependence was triggered by the absence of a human, not by the presence of AI. This pattern had been noticed in a descriptive pass before the formal analysis and is reported as post hoc. Restricting the sample to participants who believed the description (*n* = 206) left the primary contrast unchanged (genuineness Δ = 0.83, *p* = .001; warmth Δ = 0.52, *p* = .006; OriHime evaluation Δ = 0.40, *p* = .025).

*A competence penalty confined to the relational clip.* The competence rating showed the only hint of a clip-specific effect: the AI-only penalty was larger in the relational clip than in the two task clips (interaction contrast 0.33, *p* = .037), although within the relational clip alone the difference was not significant (ordinal OR = 1.78 [0.88, 3.58], *p* = .108). A position × condition interaction for competence (*p* = .012) did not decompose into any interpretable component (human × position *p* = .514; control source × position *p* = .617) and is treated as noise.

*Bookkeeping.* Of the 113 exploratory tests, 37 reached *p* < .05; 11 of these are equivalence tests (where *p* < .05 indicates equivalence) and 13 are mean-rank comparisons that are not interpretable across conditions offering different numbers of actors. Of the remaining 79 tests, 13 reached *p* < .05 against about 4 expected by chance; they cluster in the attitude-moderation block and in the sample restrictions that reproduce the primary contrast.
