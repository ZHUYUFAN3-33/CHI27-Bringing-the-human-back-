# Story C — Disclosing an operator's disability does not change how the interaction is judged

> Candidate storyline led by the disability factor as a bounded null. **Conditional on Study 2**: only viable if the clips are not already read as “no disability” by most viewers (scenarios Dc or Da; scenario Db voids the claim).

## Title candidates
- Invisible by Design: Disclosing an Avatar Robot Operator's Disability Does Not Change How the Interaction Is Judged
- Does It Matter Who Is Behind the Robot? Disability Disclosure, Control Disclosure and the Judgment of a Teleoperated Interaction
- Bringing the Human Back Without Bringing the Label Forward

## Draft abstract (≈230 words)
Avatar robots such as OriHime are operated by people with disabilities who might otherwise be excluded from workplaces. Does telling observers about the operator's disability change how they judge the robot's interactions? In an online experiment (N = 300; 272 analysed), participants watched three identical clips of OriHime talking with a coworker after reading one of seven descriptions varying who controls the robot (human, human with AI assistance, AI only) and, for human operators, whether the operator was described as having an intellectual disability, a mobility disability, or not described. Explicit disclosure of a disability produced no detectable average difference on any evaluation (two-sided p ≥ .112 on the four primary outcomes) or on attribution of responsibility and credit; the two disability descriptions did not differ from each other; no moderator was found among task type, contact with people with disabilities, or stereotype content. Effects larger than half a standard deviation are excluded and Bayes factors on participant units favour the null by 3–12 to one, while small negative effects remain compatible with the data. By contrast, describing the robot as controlled entirely by AI lowered perceived genuineness (d = .62) and warmth. We interpret the disclosure result as a bounded null for a one-sentence textual disclosure in the presence of competent behaviour, and discuss what it does and does not imply for inclusive telepresence work.

## Contributions
1. A bounded null with equivalence bounds, Bayes factors and a moderator search: explicit disclosure of an operator's disability did not detectably change evaluations or attributions.
2. The contrast case in the same experiment: disclosing “AI only” did change evaluations, so the null is not a failure of the paradigm to detect disclosure effects.
3. A methodological account of how to report and bound a null in an HCI experiment.

## Discussion themes
- Observation over label: a one-sentence disclosure did not override competent behaviour; what this does and does not say about stigma.
- Why the null is informative: bounds, Bayes factors, contrast case, uptake evidence.
- Boundary conditions: stereotype items before disclosure; equal-training assurance after it; competent clips; explicit vs visible disability.
- Study 2 context: what viewers infer with no description (default impression).

## Limitations that must lead
Single-sentence disclosure; no disability-specific recall or belief check; the “no mention” control is not “no disability”; equal-training assurance may neutralise the competence stereotype; 41% power at d = .24; not preregistered.

## Reviewer risks
- “A null from a weak manipulation is uninformative.” Answer: uptake evidence, bounds, BF, the AI-only contrast case, Study 2 default impression — and honest acknowledgement.
- “Your control condition already implies no disability.” Answer depends on Study 2 (Da vs Dc); Db would sink the paper.
- “Why not preregistered?” Pre-specified before inference; stated.

## What Study 2 must show
DIS majority “can't tell” (Dc) is the best case; majority “no” (Da) is workable with the observation-over-label frame; majority “yes” (Db) makes this storyline untenable.
