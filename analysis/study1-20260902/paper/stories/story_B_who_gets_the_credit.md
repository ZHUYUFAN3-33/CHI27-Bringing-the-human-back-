# Story B — Who gets the credit?

> Candidate storyline led by attribution (RQ4), with the perception results as the second act.

## Title candidates
- Who Gets the Credit? AI Assistance Shifts Responsibility and Credit Away from the Human Behind an Avatar Robot
- Shared Control, Shifted Blame: How Disclosed AI Involvement Redistributes Accountability for a Teleoperated Robot
- The Operator Disappears: Attribution of Responsibility and Credit When an AI Assists a Human Avatar Pilot

## Draft abstract (≈210 words)
When an AI system assists the human operating an avatar robot, who is held responsible when things go wrong, and who is credited when they go right? We showed 272 online participants three identical clips of the avatar robot OriHime interacting with a coworker after telling them that the robot was controlled by a human operator, by a human operator with AI assistance, or by an AI system alone. After each clip participants ranked the human operator, the AI, the robot's provider and the interlocutor by responsibility for a described negative outcome and by credit for a positive one. Adding AI assistance to a human operator lowered the odds that the operator was ranked first for responsibility (72% → 55%) and for credit (71% → 51%); anchored on the actors offered in every condition, the operator was ranked ahead of both the provider and the interlocutor in 72% vs 61% of rankings (OR .59 and .61). The share moved away went almost entirely to the AI. In the same participants, AI assistance did not change how genuine, warm or competent the interaction seemed, whereas describing the robot as AI-only did. Disclosing the operator's disability changed neither attribution nor evaluation. We discuss the accountability gap that opens when AI assistance is disclosed but the human's contribution is not.

## Contributions
1. Quantified redistribution of responsibility and credit under disclosed AI assistance, with rankings, Plackett–Luce worths and a common-anchor correction for the option set.
2. A dissociation: AI assistance shifts attribution without shifting perception (perception differences bounded within ±0.35 SD).
3. The blame-over-credit asymmetry for the human operator under AI assistance (small, consistent across clips).

## Introduction frame
- **Territory:** accountability in human–AI teams; moral crumple zones; credit assignment in mixed-initiative work; avatar robots operated by remote workers.
- **Gap:** attribution studies rarely hold behaviour constant across conditions or include the human operator as a candidate for credit as well as blame; telepresence work by people with disabilities raises the stakes of who is seen as doing the work.
- **Occupying:** RQ4 first (who is held responsible and credited under H, HA, A), then RQ1–RQ2 as the perceptual context.

## Results order
1. 4.1 Checks (credibility equal; CR3 responds).
2. 4.2 Attribution under H vs HA: raw ORs, common-anchor ORs, Plackett–Luce worths, second-place analysis (AI is #2 two thirds of the time) [pre-specified RQ4 + secondary].
3. 4.3 Attribution when no human is present: AI takes 57–60%; on the common anchor its standing relative to provider and interlocutor is similar to HA — the shift reflects the operator's absence [pre-specified + secondary].
4. 4.4 Blame vs credit asymmetry within HA (OR 1.21, p = .032) [secondary].
5. 4.5 Disclosure of disability leaves attribution unchanged (OR .80–.89) [pre-specified C3 on attribution].
6. 4.6 Perception: the same participants show no H–HA difference (bounded) but an AI-only penalty on genuineness and warmth [pre-specified C1, C2].
7. 4.7 Exploratory bookkeeping.

## Discussion themes
- Disclosed AI assistance opens an accountability gap: perception unchanged, credit halved.
- Implications for how AI-assisted telepresence is described to third parties: crediting the operator explicitly.
- Blame-over-credit asymmetry as a labour-recognition concern for operators with disabilities.

## Limitations that must lead
Rankings over hypothetical outcomes stated in text; the option set differs by condition (addressed by the common anchor but not eliminated); three actors in H vs four in HA; single robot and workplace; no direct manipulation check.

## Reviewer risks
- “The shift is a menu effect.” Answer: common-anchor analysis (OR .59/.61).
- “Hypothetical outcomes, not observed ones.” Acknowledge; the clips showed neither failure nor success.
- “Where are the perception effects?” They are the second act, and the dissociation is the point.
