# Story A — Presence, not profile

> Candidate storyline (the current trial storyline, extended into a full paper frame). Evidence tags: [pre-specified] [bounded] [secondary] [exploratory].

## Title candidates
- Bringing the Human Back? Disclosed Control Changes How an Avatar Robot's Interactions Feel, Its Operator's Profile Does Not
- A Human Behind the Robot: Disclosure of Control, Not of Disability, Shapes Perceptions of a Teleoperated Robot
- Who Is Behind OriHime? Presence of a Human Operator Matters More Than Who the Operator Is

## Draft abstract (≈220 words)
Avatar robots such as OriHime let people, including people with disabilities, work remotely through a robot body, increasingly with AI assistance. We asked whether what observers are *told* about who controls the robot changes how they judge an interaction they can see. In an online experiment (N = 300, 272 in the analysis sample), participants watched three identical clips of OriHime talking with a coworker after reading one of seven descriptions: a human operator, a human operator with AI assistance, or an AI system alone, with the human operator described as having an intellectual disability, a mobility disability, or not described. Describing the robot as controlled entirely by AI made the same interaction feel less genuine (d = .62), the controller less warm (d = .52) and the robot less useful, while leaving competence and trustworthiness unchanged. Adding AI assistance to a human operator changed nothing perceptibly: differences were bounded within a third of a standard deviation. Disclosing the operator's disability produced no detectable change in any evaluation or attribution, with effects bounded within half a standard deviation. What AI involvement did change was attribution: it moved responsibility and credit away from the human operator towards the AI. Exploratory analyses suggest the AI-only penalty is concentrated among participants sceptical of AI. We discuss implications for disclosure design in AI-assisted telepresence work.

## Contributions
1. An experimental isolation of *prior attribution*: identical behaviour, seven disclosures, 272 raters — showing that the description of control alone shifts perceived genuineness, warmth and usefulness.
2. Bounded null results with equivalence bounds for two things practitioners worry about: AI assistance to a human operator (no perceptual cost) and disclosure of the operator's disability (no detectable effect within this material).
3. Evidence that AI involvement redistributes responsibility and credit away from the human operator, quantified with rankings and Plackett–Luce worths, with the option-set confound addressed.

## Discussion themes
- Presence over profile: the label “no human” costs genuineness and warmth; the label “AI assists” costs nothing perceptually but shifts who is held accountable.
- Design implication: disclosure formats that keep the human visible in AI-assisted telepresence; accountability statements when AI assists.
- Disability disclosure as a bounded null: what it does and does not license (explicit, one-sentence, competent-behaviour context).
- Attitude moderation as a hypothesis for the next study.

## Limitations that must lead
No direct manipulation check (BEL1 + CR3 + attribution as indirect evidence); one small AI-only cell with larger variance; genuineness item wording close to the manipulation; clip length confounded with task; minimal disclosure preceded by stereotype items and followed by a training assurance; US online sample; analysis plan pre-specified but not preregistered.
