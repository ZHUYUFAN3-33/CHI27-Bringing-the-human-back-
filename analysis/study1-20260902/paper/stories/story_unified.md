# Unified storyline — Bringing the Human Back?

> Status: **adopted 2026-09-05** as the single frame for the CHI 2027 manuscript. It combines four questions—how disclosed control source changes evaluation, how AI involvement redistributes responsibility and credit, how disability disclosure changes judgment, and, exploratorily, whose response to an AI-only description is most negative—rather than treating them as competing headlines. Structure proposed by the PI; ordering and framing rules below are the analyst's adjustments, marked ▲. For PI confirmation, use `story_for_pi_review.md`, which does not require any internal codes.

## One-line thesis
When observers judge a teleoperated avatar robot at work, describing a human as present preserves genuineness and warmth even when AI assists; AI involvement nevertheless shifts responsibility and credit away from the operator, while disability disclosure and favourable evaluation coexist without an observable penalty under the conditions tested.

## Selected title
**Bringing the Human Back? How Disclosing Who Controls an Avatar Robot Shapes Judgments of Genuineness, Warmth and Responsibility**

Selected by the PI on 2026-09-05. The title is fixed for the next pipeline phase, but its “bringing back” mechanism must still be worded according to the independent no-disclosure baseline when those data exist.

## Why this problem is important

Avatar robots can turn remote embodiment into paid work and social participation for people whose bodies cannot easily enter conventional workplaces. This makes the disclosure of who is behind the robot consequential: it can determine whether a human contribution is visible, who receives responsibility and credit, and whether an operator can make a disability known without being judged less favourably. As AI begins to assist or replace parts of the control loop, the same competent behaviour may be understood as human work, human–AI work or AI performance solely because of a short description. The paper therefore concerns recognition, agency and inclusion in an emerging form of work—not simply whether observers like robots or AI.

## What prior work establishes—and the missing link

- **Avatar work and operator experience.** OriHime research documents fulfilment, accessible forms of work and operator-controlled identity presentation (Takeuchi et al., 2020; Hatada et al., 2024), while telepresence reviews describe accessibility benefits. This evidence is predominantly operator-side; it does not establish how third-party observers judge the same avatar-mediated performance under different descriptions.
- **AI disclosure and evaluation.** Text, voice and bot studies show that AI disclosure or suspected AI authorship can reduce purchasing, trust or cooperation (Luo et al., 2019; Jakesch et al., 2019). The closest robot field study linked unrecognised teleoperation to higher service ratings, but neither recognition nor behaviour was experimentally fixed (Baba et al., 2020). An embodied identical-behaviour comparison of human-only control, human control with AI assistance and AI-only control remains missing.
- **Responsibility and credit.** Work on AI-mediated communication, automation and AI-generated art shows that autonomy and framing can shift blame or credit between humans and AI (Elish, 2019; Hohenstein & Jung, 2020; Epstein et al., 2020). Credit is less studied than blame, and the searched literature does not jointly map responsibility and credit across an avatar operator, AI, robot provider and interaction partner.
- **Disability disclosure and judgment.** Disability research documents warmth–competence stereotypes and disclosure-strategy effects in workplaces (Fiske et al., 2002; Rohmer & Louvet, 2018; Lyons et al., 2017). It does not establish whether intellectual- or mobility-disability disclosure changes third-party judgments of an identical, visibly competent avatar-mediated performance, and relevant null findings are rarely bounded with equivalence tests and Bayes factors.

Within the documented 83-record search corpus, no randomised identical-behaviour experiment connects all four literatures in a physically embodied avatar robot. The manuscript's argument chain is therefore: **avatar work creates access → AI creates uncertainty about the human contribution → disclosure can change evaluation, attribution and identity-related judgment → prior literatures address these effects separately → this experiment identifies how they operate together.**

## Section frame (PI structure, with adjustments ▲)

### 1 Introduction
- **1.1 Real-world importance.** Remote operation and telepresence let people work in places their bodies cannot reach. OriHime (OryLab, Tokyo) turns this into employment: operators with disabilities serve customers and join meetings through the robot. Existing avatar-work research shows benefits for access, fulfilment and identity presentation, making the human behind the robot—not the machine alone—the socially consequential unit. AI assistance is now entering the same loop through speech support, shared autonomy and generated responses. Customers and coworkers may therefore need a written disclosure to know whether the behaviour comes from a human operator, a human assisted by AI or AI alone.
- **1.2 What is known, what is missing and why it matters.** Prior research separately shows that avatar work can support operators, AI labels can change evaluation, automation can redistribute blame or credit, and disability disclosure can activate workplace stereotypes. What it does not show is how these processes combine when observers see the same competent embodied behaviour. A short description can make the human visible or invisible, shape who deserves credit or blame, and disclose an operator's disability. The practical question is whether AI-supported avatar work preserves the operator's recognition, agency and control over self-presentation.
  - **Disclosed control source and evaluation:** Does describing human-only, human-with-AI or AI-only control change how the interaction and controller are judged?
  - **Responsibility and credit under AI involvement:** When AI is disclosed as involved, who is held responsible and who is credited?
  - **Disability disclosure and judgment:** Does disclosing that the operator has a disability change evaluations and attributions?
  - **Attitude-dependent responses to AI-only control (exploratory):** For whom does the AI-only description matter most?
- **1.3 Integrated gap and contributions.** Scope the novelty claim to the documented 83-record search corpus: no randomised study of a physically embodied avatar robot has held behaviour constant while jointly manipulating human-only, human-with-AI and AI-only descriptions, mapping responsibility and credit across multiple actors, and testing operator-disability disclosure. Contributions: (1) an identical-behaviour experiment that isolates disclosure; (2) evidence that human presence, not AI assistance or operator profile, drives genuineness and warmth; (3) evidence that attribution shifts to AI even where perception does not; and (4) encouraging but bounded evidence that disability disclosure and favourable evaluation can coexist in avatar-mediated work, supported by equivalence bounds and Bayes factors. The planned independent no-disclosure baseline is not advertised as a contribution until observed data show that it materially clarifies one of these claims.
- ▲ The questions and contributions close the Introduction, before Related Work (CHI convention); the literature then answers "what is known" for each question.

### 2 Related work
- **2.1 Avatar robots and avatar work.** Known: OriHime and telepresence studies document access, social participation, operator fulfilment and identity work. Missing: the evidence is mainly operator-side and small-scale; third-party judgments of an operator's identical performance remain untested. Why it matters: access to work is incomplete if the operator's contribution becomes socially invisible.
- **2.2 Disclosing automation and perceiving an interaction.** Known: Wizard-of-Oz, bot-disclosure, mind-perception and algorithm-response research shows that labels and beliefs about agency can change trust, warmth, cooperation and purchasing. Missing: prior studies are mostly text or voice, usually compare human with AI, and do not randomise a three-level control-source description around identical embodied avatar behaviour. Why it matters: AI assistance may be disclosed without erasing human presence—or may make a human contribution harder to recognise.
- **2.3 Responsibility and credit in human–AI teams.** Known: moral-crumple-zone, robot, driving, chat and AI-art research shows that perceived autonomy redistributes attribution. Missing: credit is less studied than blame, and responsibility and credit have not been jointly ranked across the operator, AI, provider and interaction partner in AI-assisted avatar work. Why it matters: positive evaluations can coexist with the loss of recognition for human labour.
- **2.4 Disability disclosure, stigma and stereotype content.** Known: disability can evoke mixed warmth–competence stereotypes, and disclosure strategy changes workplace judgments; contact can reduce prejudice. Missing: no large controlled perceiver-side study in the corpus fixes competent avatar behaviour while varying intellectual- and mobility-disability disclosure, and prior nulls are rarely bounded. Why it matters: operators should be able to control self-disclosure without an avoidable evaluative or attributional cost.
- **2.5 Integrated gap.** Bring the three outcome processes together—evaluation of the interaction, allocation of responsibility and credit, and judgment following disability disclosure—under one experimentally controlled disclosure design. End with the four fully named questions and preserve the 83-record corpus qualifier.

### 3 Method
Between-subjects 3 × 2 + 1: human-only and human-with-AI descriptions were each crossed with no disability mention, intellectual-disability disclosure and mobility-disability disclosure, plus one AI-only condition. Participants watched three clips (casual conversation, product recommendation and collaborative task) in six orders; N = 300, primary n = 272 after attention and video checks. Measures covered overall evaluation, genuineness, warmth, competence, perceived control, description credibility, responsibility and credit rankings, AI attitudes, robot attitudes, stereotype content and prior disability contact. The pre-specified analysis used mixed models with participant random intercepts, theory-driven contrasts, within-family Holm correction, equivalence bounds set at the design's minimum detectable effects, participant-unit BIC Bayes factors and a participant-cluster bootstrap for the higher-variance AI-only condition. State plainly: pre-specified before inference, not preregistered.

### 4 Results (▲ semantic order rather than internal letter codes)
- **4.1 Checks.** Attention/video exclusions; description credibility equal across conditions (p .451); perceived control provides evidence that the human-only versus human-with-AI description was processed (d .40); disclosure-page dwell time increased by 5.5 seconds when disability was disclosed.
- **4.2 How disclosed control source changes evaluation.** The omnibus condition effect was significant for perceived genuineness (p .004), marginal for controller warmth, and not significant for overall evaluation or competence. Human-described versus AI-only control differed by d = .62 on genuineness in every specification, by d = .52 on warmth, and on the usefulness item within overall evaluation. Human-only and human-with-AI descriptions were statistically bounded within ±.35/±.50 standard deviations. Ten of 84 primary pairwise comparisons had uncorrected p < .05, every one placed a human-described condition above AI-only, and none of 60 comparisons among human-described conditions differed; bootstrap results agreed despite 1.3–1.5 times larger standard deviations in the AI-only condition.
- **4.3 How AI involvement redistributes responsibility and credit.** Ranked-first models show that AI involvement lowers the odds that the operator is ranked first for responsibility and credit (odds ratios .47/.42; .59/.61 on the common-anchor measure). Under human control with AI assistance, the operator was ranked first for blame more often than for credit (+4.8 percentage points, p = .024), so the asymmetry runs against the human.
- **4.4 How disability disclosure changes judgment and attribution.** Disclosed disability versus no mention produced no significant difference on the four primary outcomes (p ≥ .11); point estimates were small and slightly negative (largest d ≈ −.24), equivalence tests excluded effects of half a standard deviation or larger, and participant-unit Bayes factors ranged from 3 to 12 in favour of the null. Intellectual- and mobility-disability disclosures did not differ, the pooled result was the same, none of the prespecified or exploratory contextual variables changed this pattern, and attribution was unchanged (odds ratios .80–.89). Longer disclosure-page dwell time provides evidence that the additional sentence was read. Positively framed, disability disclosure and favourable evaluation coexisted in this setting without a detectable evaluative or attributional penalty.
- **4.5 Whose response to AI-only control is most negative (exploratory).** The AI-only evaluation penalty was concentrated among participants with less positive AI attitudes (interaction p = .019 for overall evaluation and p = .003 for perceived competence; simple slopes reported). The usefulness item carried the overall-evaluation difference, while a clip-specific competence pattern remained only a weak lead. Across 113 exploratory tests, 37 had p < .05 and none was promoted to a primary finding. This exploratory moderation remains outside the abstract.

### Conditional evidence module — independent no-disclosure baseline
- **Naming.** `Study 2`, branch `study2` and the `S2_` item prefix are internal identifiers only. In the manuscript call it the **independent no-disclosure baseline** or **no-disclosure baseline**.
- **Purpose.** The primary experiment alone cannot say whether each description agreed with, cut against, or filled a blank in what the clips imply on their own. A fresh cohort (internal instrument version s2-v4, target ~300 usable) sees the same clips without a control-source or operator-profile description and reports perceived control source, inferred operator disability and genuineness, each with confidence, followed by prior OriHime familiarity.
- **Inferential boundary.** This baseline is not a replication, causal robustness test, second experiment or mediation analysis. Every comparison is cross-cohort and interpretive.
- **Placement.** No standalone section is reserved before results. Full methods/results go in supplementary material. ## Framing rules for disability disclosure (the "is the null good news?" question)
The PI asked whether the non-significant disability result can be presented as a good thing ("more inclusive?"). The manuscript leads with its positive implication and then states its boundaries:
1. **Preferred positive claim:** "Disability disclosure and favourable evaluation coexisted under the conditions tested: disclosing the operator's disability produced no detectable cost in how the interaction was judged or in who was credited, and effects of half a standard deviation or larger were excluded."
2. **Allowed with the boundary stated:** "A one-line disclosure did not override what participants saw; judgments followed the observed interaction, not the label"—but only if the no-disclosure baseline shows a clear no-disability default or substantial uncertainty. If the baseline instead shows a clear disability default, the sentence becomes "explicit disclosure added no detectable incremental effect beyond an impression the clips already gave".
3. **Positive implication allowed:** the result provides encouraging evidence for designing more inclusive avatar-mediated work because it identifies a setting in which operators with disabilities may disclose their disability without being judged less favourably. This is an implication from the measured third-party judgments, not a direct measure of workplace inclusion.
4. **Not allowed:** "The workplace is more inclusive", "disclosure has no effect", or "there is no stigma". A null is not proof of absence: effects to about d = −.24 remain compatible with the data, and the equal-training sentence may have neutralised the competence stereotype.
5. **Recommended paragraph:** "Encouragingly, disclosing the operator's disability did not produce a detectable evaluative or attributional penalty under the conditions tested. This suggests that avatar-mediated work may offer a setting in which operators with disabilities can disclose their disability without being judged less favourably—at least when observers see competent performance and are told that the operator received equivalent training. Although this does not establish that stigma is absent, that disability disclosure never matters, or that workplaces are already inclusive, it identifies a concrete condition under which disability disclosure and favourable evaluation can coexist."

## Defensibility
High for the control-source evaluation and responsibility/credit findings (robust across specifications and survive correction); medium for the positive but bounded claim that disability disclosure and favourable evaluation coexisted, conditional on the no-disclosure baseline not showing that disability was already the default inference; low for the attitude-dependent AI-only response, which remains exploratory and excluded from the abstract.
