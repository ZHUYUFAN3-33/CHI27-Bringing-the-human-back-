# Story for PI Review — Bringing the Human Back?

Status: prepared for PI confirmation on 2026-09-05. This document uses full semantic names rather than internal storyline, condition, contrast, outcome, or scenario codes.

Editable story-logic diagram: [Bringing the Human Back — Self-Contained Story Logic](https://www.figma.com/board/oeTwQppYyLOcU28wDPo3dl)

## Proposed title

**Bringing the Human Back? How Disclosing Who Controls an Avatar Robot Shapes Judgments of Genuineness, Warmth and Responsibility**

## Why this problem matters

Avatar robots are not only communication devices. They can provide a route into paid work and social participation for people who cannot easily enter a conventional workplace. Studies of OriHime operators have documented fulfilment, manageable forms of work and new ways to present one's identity through an avatar. As AI begins to assist—or potentially replace—parts of the operator's work, however, the person contributing through the robot can become harder for customers and coworkers to see.

That visibility has practical consequences. A short description of who controls the robot may change whether an otherwise identical interaction feels genuine or warm, whether the human operator receives credit or blame, and whether disclosing a disability introduces a social cost. These are not merely questions about whether people like AI. They concern whether AI-supported avatar work preserves the human operator's agency, recognition and ability to decide how they are represented at work.

## What prior work already tells us—and what remains unknown

### Avatar work creates access and supports identity, but perceiver-side evidence is scarce

Research with OriHime operators shows that avatar-mediated work can support social participation and fulfilment (Takeuchi et al., 2020), while qualitative work shows that operators use avatars to negotiate identity and what they reveal about themselves (Hatada et al., 2024). A systematic review similarly finds accessibility benefits across telepresence research. Yet this literature is predominantly operator-side. Within our documented 83-record search corpus, it does not show how customers or coworkers judge the same competent avatar-mediated performance when the operator is described differently.

### AI disclosure can change evaluation, but prior studies do not isolate this question in avatar work

Bot, voice and text studies show that identifying AI can reduce purchasing, trust or cooperation, and that uncertainty about whether a human or AI produced content can itself matter (e.g., Luo et al., 2019; Jakesch et al., 2019). The closest robot field study found that customers who did not realise a service robot was teleoperated evaluated it more favourably, but that realisation was not randomised and the behaviour was not held fixed (Baba et al., 2020). What remains missing is an embodied test that randomises three descriptions—human-only control, human control with AI assistance and AI-only control—while every participant sees the same behaviour.

### Automation can redistribute blame and credit, but assisted avatar work has not been mapped as a multi-actor system

Prior studies of AI-mediated chat, robots, driving and AI-generated art show that autonomy and framing can move responsibility or credit between humans and AI (e.g., Hohenstein & Jung, 2020; Elish, 2019; Epstein et al., 2020). However, blame has received more attention than credit. Within our search corpus, no study jointly ranks both responsibility and credit across the operator, AI, robot provider and interaction partner in AI-assisted avatar work. This matters because an interaction can still be evaluated positively even while the human's contribution becomes less visible.

### Disability research documents stigma and disclosure effects, but not this fixed-performance setting

Prior research documents warmth–competence stereotypes of people with disabilities and shows that workplace responses can depend on how a disability is disclosed (Fiske et al., 2002; Rohmer & Louvet, 2018; Lyons et al., 2017). What it does not establish is how third-party observers respond when an intellectual or mobility disability is disclosed alongside the same visibly competent robot-mediated performance. The literature also rarely uses equivalence tests or Bayes factors to distinguish an inconclusive nonsignificant result from evidence that rules out effects of a specified size.

## The integrated gap this experiment addresses

These literatures have largely studied separate pieces of the problem: what avatar work means to operators, how people react to AI labels, how automation affects blame or credit, and how disability disclosure affects workplace judgment. Within our documented 83-record search corpus, no randomised identical-behaviour experiment brings the pieces together for a physically embodied avatar robot. Our design therefore asks whether the same disclosure simultaneously changes evaluation, recognition of human agency and the interpersonal consequences of making an operator's disability known.

## The central story

Our experiment held the observed behaviour constant and changed only the description of who controlled the robot and, when a human was present, whether the operator's disability was disclosed. The central result is that describing a human as present mattered more than describing what kind of human was present or whether that human received AI assistance. At the same time, merely introducing AI into the description redistributed responsibility and credit away from the operator. The paper therefore connects two processes that do not move in parallel: observers may evaluate human-only and AI-assisted interaction similarly while assigning agency differently. Encouragingly, disability disclosure and favourable evaluation coexisted under the conditions tested, identifying a concrete setting in which making an operator's disability known did not carry an observable interpersonal penalty.

## Questions addressed by the paper

1. **How does disclosing the controller change judgments of the interaction?**  
   Does describing the same interaction as human-controlled, human-controlled with AI assistance, or AI-controlled change perceived genuineness, warmth, competence and usefulness?

2. **How does disclosed AI involvement redistribute responsibility and credit?**  
   When AI assistance is introduced, do observers continue to treat the human operator as the principal source of the robot's actions and outcomes?

3. **How does disclosing the operator's disability affect judgments and attribution?**  
   Does explicitly identifying an intellectual or mobility disability change evaluations of the same competent performance or change who receives responsibility and credit?

4. **Exploratory: for whom does an AI-only description matter most?**  
   Do participants' prior attitudes toward AI help explain why some respond more negatively than others to an AI-only description?

The fourth question is retained as a labelled exploratory analysis in Results and Discussion. It is **not included in the abstract**.

## Results narrative

### Human presence matters more than AI assistance or operator profile

Describing the controller as AI alone lowered perceived genuineness by approximately 0.62 standard deviations and lowered perceived controller warmth by approximately 0.52 standard deviations. In contrast, describing a human operator with AI assistance produced no detectable evaluation cost relative to describing a human operator without AI assistance; the two were statistically bounded within the prespecified equivalence intervals. Across the pairwise comparisons, every difference that remained significant after Holm adjustment (adjusted p < .05) involved the AI-only condition; no adjusted-significant difference appeared among conditions that described a human as present.

The intended interpretation is not simply that “people dislike AI.” The more defensible claim is that preserving a human presence in the description maintained genuineness and warmth, even when AI assistance was explicitly acknowledged.

### Responsibility and credit move before evaluations do

Although human-only and AI-assisted interactions were evaluated similarly, disclosing AI assistance reduced how often the human operator was ranked first for responsibility and credit. The operator's first-place share fell from roughly 71–72% under human-only control to 51–55% under human control with AI assistance. Under AI assistance, participants also ranked the operator first for blame more often than for credit, a difference of 4.8 percentage points.

This creates the paper's main conceptual tension: AI assistance can leave perceptions of the interaction intact while making the human contribution less visible. The shift in attribution therefore begins before an evaluation penalty appears.

### Disability disclosure and favourable evaluation coexisted in this setting

Disclosing an intellectual or mobility disability produced no detectable change in genuineness, warmth, competence, usefulness, responsibility or credit. Point estimates were small, with the largest standardised difference around −0.24. Equivalence tests excluded effects of half a standard deviation or larger, while participant-level Bayes factors generally favoured the null to varying degrees.

Encouragingly, disclosing the operator's disability did not produce a detectable evaluative or attributional penalty under the conditions tested. This suggests that avatar-mediated work may offer a setting in which operators with disabilities can disclose their disability without being judged less favourably—at least when observers see competent performance and are told that the operator received equivalent training. Although this does not establish that stigma is absent, that disability disclosure never matters, or that workplaces are already inclusive, it identifies a concrete condition under which disability disclosure and favourable evaluation can coexist. This is the paper's positive but bounded inclusion-related interpretation.

### Exploratory heterogeneity by prior attitudes toward AI

The negative response to the AI-only description was concentrated among participants with less positive prior attitudes toward AI. Because this moderation emerged from a large exploratory analysis family, it is reported transparently as a clue rather than elevated to a headline contribution. It motivates audience-aware disclosure as a tentative design question, not a settled recommendation.

This exploratory result appears in the Results and Discussion only. It does **not** appear in the abstract.

## What the abstract will and will not contain

Within the 150-word limit, the abstract will contain:

1. the avatar-work and AI-assistance context;
2. the experiment with identical behaviour and seven controller/operator descriptions;
3. the finding that an AI-only description reduced genuineness and warmth, whereas adding AI assistance to a human operator did not;
4. the finding that AI involvement redistributed responsibility and credit away from the operator; and
5. the encouraging but bounded finding that disability disclosure and favourable evaluation coexisted, with no detectable evaluation or attribution cost in this setting.

The abstract will not contain the exploratory moderation by prior AI attitudes. It will also not promise findings from the independent no-disclosure baseline before those data exist.

## Role of the future independent no-disclosure baseline

The pending fresh-cohort work is publicly called the **independent no-disclosure baseline**. It is not presented as a second experiment, replication, robustness test or causal comparison. Its purpose is to estimate what viewers infer from the same clips when they receive no description of the controller or operator.

Complete baseline methods and results will remain in the supplementary material. A short subsection will enter the end of the main Results only if the observed baseline materially changes whether the primary experiment is best interpreted as an AI-label cost, a human-presence benefit, a deviation from a human–AI default, or disclosure filling an informational blank. Otherwise the main paper will use only a brief contextual statement or a limitation.

## Discussion structure

1. **Human presence matters more than operator profile.** What describing a human as present preserves, and why disclosed AI assistance did not carry the same evaluation cost as AI-only control.
2. **Responsibility and credit migrate before perception does.** Why AI assistance can leave evaluations unchanged while reducing the visibility of human contribution.
3. **Disability disclosure without an observable penalty.** How the coexistence of disclosure and favourable evaluation can inform more inclusive avatar-mediated work, together with its material, measurement and generalisation limits.
4. **Who is most affected by an AI-only description?** The exploratory attitude pattern, explicitly separated from the primary claims.
5. **Default impressions as an interpretive boundary.** Included only if the independent no-disclosure baseline provides informative evidence.
6. **Design implications.** Keep the human contribution visible, credit operators in AI-assisted work, use the no-observable-penalty result to motivate more inclusive avatar-mediated work while leaving disability disclosure under operator control, treat audience-aware disclosure as tentative, and report null effects with explicit bounds.
