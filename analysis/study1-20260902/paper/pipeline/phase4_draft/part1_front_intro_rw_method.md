# Bringing the Human Back? How Disclosing Who Controls an Avatar Robot Shapes Judgments of Genuineness, Warmth and Responsibility

[Authors and affiliations: PLACEHOLDER]

## Abstract

Avatar robots such as OriHime let people with severe disabilities work through a robot body, and AI assistance is entering the same control loop, so the people who meet the robot learn who is behind it from a sentence. In an online experiment (N = 300; 272 analysed), participants watched three identical clips of OriHime talking with a coworker after reading one of seven descriptions that varied the controller (human operator, human operator with AI assistance, AI system alone) and, for human operators, whether an intellectual or mobility-related disability was disclosed. The AI-only description lowered perceived genuineness (d = 0.62) and warmth (d = 0.52); AI assistance to a human did not, within ±0.35 SD. Describing AI as involved nonetheless shifted responsibility and credit away from the operator. Disclosing the operator's disability produced no detectable evaluative or attributional penalty, with effects of half a standard deviation or more excluded. Disclosure should keep the human visible and credited.

**CCS Concepts:** Human-centered computing → Empirical studies in HCI; Accessibility; Human-centered computing → Collaborative and social computing.

**Keywords:** avatar robot, telepresence, teleoperation, AI disclosure, attribution of responsibility, disability disclosure, OriHime, equivalence testing

## 1 Introduction

### 1.1 Why avatar-mediated work matters

Telepresence lets people work in places their bodies cannot easily reach. Mobile remote presence systems gave remote employees a body in the office and changed how colleagues treated them; the robot came to stand for the person piloting it [@lee2011body]. In Japan, the avatar robot OriHime has turned this idea into employment. Operators with severe physical disabilities, some of them bed-bound, serve customers and take part in meetings through the robot, and a pilot study of café work with OriHime-D reported fulfilment, workloads matched to each operator's capacity, and a form of work suited to people seeking social participation [@takeuchi2020avatar]. A longitudinal case study at the same café found that operators used their avatars to renegotiate identity and to decide what to reveal about themselves [@hatada2024redefining]. A systematic review of telepresence robots for people with special needs reached a similar conclusion at a larger scale: such robots can raise quality of life through remote social interaction, while access barriers and open questions about autonomy remain [@zhang2022telepresence]. National research programmes now aim at "cybernetic avatars" that transmit an operator's intentions with increasing autonomous support [@ishiguro2021avatar].

That last phrase marks a change. AI assistance, whether speech support, suggested wording or shared control of movement, is entering the loop between operator and robot. A robot whose voice is generated and whose movements are smoothed looks the same whether a person or a program is choosing what it says. The customers and coworkers who meet an avatar robot therefore rarely know from observation who is acting through it. They are usually told, in a sentence on a sign, a website or an introduction: "operated by a team member", "AI-assisted", "powered by AI".

That sentence carries more than a technical fact. It decides whether the human behind the robot is visible at all. It implies who deserves credit when the interaction goes well and who is answerable when it goes badly. And when the operator has a disability, it can also decide whether that fact is known to the people they serve. For a workforce whose access to employment runs through the robot, these are questions about recognition, agency and self-presentation, not only about attitudes to AI. The people who meet an avatar robot therefore judge not only what it does, but who they believe is acting through it.

### 1.2 What is known, what is missing, and why the questions belong together

Four literatures each hold one piece of the problem. Studies of avatar work show that operating a robot can bring fulfilment and control over self-presentation [@takeuchi2020avatar; @hatada2024redefining]. Studies of AI disclosure show that identical output is judged differently once people learn that an AI produced it: disclosed chatbots sold less than undisclosed ones [@luo2019machines], bots lost their cooperative advantage once revealed [@ishowooloko2019transparency], and messages labelled as AI-generated were dispreferred regardless of content [@karinshak2023persuade]. Studies of attribution show that automation redistributes blame and credit: humans working alongside automated systems absorb blame like a crumple zone [@elish2019moral; @hohenstein2020crumple], and greater machine autonomy moves credit and blame toward the machine [@kim2006blame; @epstein2020credit]. Studies of disability show that people with disabilities are stereotyped as warm but not competent, more so in work contexts, and that how a disability is disclosed can change hiring intentions [@fiske2002model; @rohmer2018implicit; @lyons2017disclosing].

What none of these literatures shows is how the three consequences of a single disclosure sentence operate together when observers see identical, competent behaviour. Does telling people that a human is present change how the same interaction feels? Does telling them that AI assists change who is credited, even if the interaction feels the same? Does telling them that the operator has a disability change either judgment? These questions share one manipulation and one observer, and the answers matter jointly: a system that preserves warm evaluations while quietly transferring credit to the AI, or that lets operators work only if they hide who they are, has not brought the human back.

### 1.3 Integrated gap, research questions and contributions

Within the 83 records we screened, no randomised study of a physically embodied avatar robot has held behaviour constant while comparing human-only, human-with-AI and AI-only descriptions, jointly mapped responsibility and credit across the operator, the AI, the robot's provider and the interaction partner, and tested the disclosure of an intellectual or a mobility-related disability. We designed such an experiment around four questions:

- **RQ1.** How does the disclosed control source (human-only, human with AI assistance, AI-only) change judgments of the same interaction and of its controller?
- **RQ2.** When AI is disclosed as involved, who is held responsible for the robot's failures and credited for its successes?
- **RQ3.** Does disclosing that the operator has an intellectual or a mobility-related disability change those judgments and attributions?
- **RQ4 (exploratory).** For whom does an AI-only description matter most?

The paper makes four contributions. First, an identical-behaviour experiment (N = 300) that isolates the effect of disclosure on judgments of an avatar robot at work. Second, evidence that human presence in the description, not AI assistance and not operator profile, drives perceived genuineness and warmth. Third, evidence that describing AI as involved redistributes responsibility and credit away from the operator even where evaluations do not change. Fourth, a bounded result for disability disclosure: disclosure and favourable evaluation coexisted without a detectable evaluative or attributional penalty, reported with equivalence bounds, Bayes factors and a moderator search rather than as a bare null. We first review what each literature establishes, then show the missing connection among them.

## 2 Related Work

### 2.1 Avatar robots and avatar work

Telepresence robots can support participation from a distance. Lee and Takayama's field study of mobile remote presence found that remote workers came to live and work with local colleagues almost as if physically present, and that new social norms formed around the robot body [@lee2011body]. For people with special needs, a systematic review of 42 studies concluded that telepresence robots can improve quality of life through remote social interaction, while noting persistent interface barriers and unresolved questions about autonomy, evaluation methods and training [@zhang2022telepresence]. OriHime is the most developed case of telepresence as employment. Takeuchi, Yamazaki and Yoshifuji piloted café service by ten operators with severe disabilities and reported mental fulfilment and workloads tailored to individual capacity [@takeuchi2020avatar]; Hatada and colleagues followed seven operators over time and showed that avatar customisation became a means of renegotiating identity and choosing what to disclose [@hatada2024redefining]. The programme vision behind such systems, cybernetic avatars with growing autonomous support, is explicit that AI will increasingly share the work [@ishiguro2021avatar].

What is missing is the perceiver's side. The avatar-work evidence is operator-side, qualitative and small (n = 7 to 10). The closest perceiver-side study found that café customers who did not realise a robot was teleoperated rated its service higher than those who did, but realisation was not randomised and the robot's behaviour was not held fixed [@baba2020teleoperated]. Within our corpus, no large controlled study asks how customers or coworkers judge the same competent avatar-mediated performance when the operator is described differently. This matters because access to a workplace does not guarantee that the operator's contribution will be recognised or evaluated fairly once AI enters the loop.

### 2.2 Disclosing automation and the perception of an interaction

Observers infer minds. Mind perception organises along agency and experience, robots are granted moderate agency and little experience, and perceived agency predicts attributions of responsibility [@gray2007dimensions]. Wizard-of-Oz practice in human–robot interaction rests on the same fact: a robot secretly controlled by a person is treated as if autonomous, and the deception is often under-reported [@riek2012wizard]. When the source is disclosed, judgments move. Undisclosed chatbots matched proficient human sales agents, but disclosure before the conversation cut purchases by 79.7 percent through perceptions of the bot as less knowledgeable and less empathetic [@luo2019machines]. Bots elicited more cooperation than humans only while believed to be human [@ishowooloko2019transparency]. People abandon algorithms after seeing them err, even when they outperform humans [@dietvorst2015algorithm]. In AI-mediated communication, hosts whose profile text was suspected to be AI-written were trusted less, although the penalty vanished when every profile carried the label [@jakesch2019aimc]; trust in an email writer fell when AI involvement was disclosed, with an unexpected reversal for interpersonal messages [@liu2022console]; and messages labelled AI-generated were dispreferred even when rated more effective [@karinshak2023persuade].

What is missing is an embodied, three-level test. Prior work concerns text, voice and chat, and nearly always contrasts human with AI. The one robot result did not randomise disclosure or fix behaviour [@baba2020teleoperated]. No study in our corpus holds the behaviour of a physically embodied avatar robot constant while varying human-only, human-with-AI and AI-only descriptions. The three-level design matters because it can separate two accounts that the two-level literature cannot: a cost of acknowledging AI assistance, and a cost of removing the human from the description altogether.

### 2.3 Responsibility and credit in human–AI teams

When people and automated systems act together, attribution shifts. Elish's account of moral crumple zones describes how responsibility for an automated system's behaviour is misattributed to the human with the least control over it [@elish2019moral]. In AI-mediated chat, smart replies raised interpersonal trust, and when a conversation failed, blame moved to the AI and away from the human partner [@hohenstein2020crumple]. In early human–robot experiments, greater robot autonomy increased the credit and blame assigned to the robot and reduced attributions to the people present [@kim2006blame]; in vignette studies, an autonomous robot drew almost as much blame as a human and a non-autonomous one almost as little as the environment [@furlough2021blame]. When a driver and an automated car both err, the driver is blamed more and the machine less [@awad2020drivers]. Credit follows the same logic: framing an AI as more agentic moves credit for AI-generated art toward the AI and away from the humans involved [@epstein2020credit]. Research agendas for AI-mediated communication list attribution among the central open questions [@hancock2020aimc].

What is missing is a joint map for assisted work. Blame is far better studied than credit, and no screened study ranks both responsibility and credit across the operator, the AI, the robot's provider and the interaction partner when a human operator is described as AI-assisted. This matters because an interaction may remain warmly evaluated while the human loses recognition for their labour, or keeps a disproportionate share of the blame.

### 2.4 Disability disclosure, stigma and stereotype content

The stereotype content model places people with disabilities, with older adults, in the paternalised quadrant of high warmth and low competence [@fiske2002model]. Implicit measures show the same pattern and sharpen it at work: people with disabilities were associated with less warmth than people without, and with less competence when a work context was primed [@rohmer2018implicit]. Disclosure is itself a strategic act. In hiring experiments, the effectiveness of a disclosure strategy depended on whether the applicant was seen as responsible for the disability's onset, with de-emphasising strategies raising pity and lowering hiring intentions in that case [@lyons2017disclosing]. Attitudes toward intellectual disability in particular are shaped by age, education and prior contact, and public knowledge is limited [@scior2011public]. Intergroup contact reliably reduces prejudice, with effects that generalise beyond the contact situation [@pettigrew2006meta].

What is missing is a perceiver-side test with performance held fixed. No large controlled study in our corpus varies whether an intellectual or a mobility-related disability is disclosed while every observer watches the same visibly competent avatar-mediated work. Disclosure and stigma studies also rarely use equivalence tests or Bayes factors, so a non-significant result cannot be distinguished from evidence that rules out effects of a given size [@lakens2017equivalence; @wagenmakers2007practical]. This matters because operators should be able to control their self-presentation without an avoidable cost, and because positive evidence, if it comes, must be stated with the conditions under which it was obtained.

### 2.5 The integrated gap

Prior work addresses operator experience, reactions to AI labels, the redistribution of blame or credit, and disability stigma as separate problems. Within the 83 records we screened, none connects them in one randomised experiment in which an embodied avatar robot behaves identically for every observer. The experiment reported next addresses evaluation, attribution and disability disclosure in one controlled design.

## 3 Method

### 3.1 Design

The experiment used a between-participants 3 × 2 + 1 design with three within-participant clips. Human-only control and human control with AI assistance were each paired with three operator descriptions (no mention of disability, an intellectual disability, a mobility-related disability), and a seventh condition described control by an AI system alone. Operator profile is undefined when no operator exists, so the AI-only condition is a single reference cell rather than a third row of the factorial. Every participant watched the same three clips in one of six counterbalanced orders; the server assigned participants to the 42 condition × order cells by a least-filled-cell rule. Participants never saw the operator.

### 3.2 Participants

Of 316 people who opened the study on CloudResearch Connect, 300 completed it; one was screened out for being unable to play video with sound and 15 left before the end. The analysis sample comprises the 272 completers who passed both quality checks built into the questionnaire: an instructed-response attention item on the second clip page (21 failures) and a four-option comprehension question about the first clip (8 failures, one of whom also failed the attention item). Cell sizes ranged from 36 (AI-only) to 41. Participants were 18 to 78 years old (M = 41.2, SD = 13.8), 50 percent women, and resident in the United States (239), Canada (27), the United Kingdom (5) and Ireland (1). Median completion time was 15.3 minutes (interquartile range 12.7 to 19.7). Participants gave informed consent and were paid at the platform's standard rate; the study was approved by [ETHICS BODY AND APPROVAL NUMBER: PLACEHOLDER].

### 3.3 Materials

*Robot and clips.* OriHime is a 23-cm tabletop avatar robot with a camera, microphone, speaker and movable head and arms, operated remotely by a person who sees and hears through it. Three pre-recorded clips showed OriHime, placed on a desk, talking with the same coworker: a relational exchange (70 s), an advice-giving exchange (105 s) and a collaborative task (115 s). The footage was identical in every condition; only the written description differed.

*Descriptions.* Before the first clip, participants read one of three control-source descriptions. Human-only control: "The OriHime here is controlled in real time by a trained human operator. The human operator chooses what OriHime says and does. No AI system generates responses or makes decisions." Human control with AI assistance: "The OriHime here is controlled by a trained human operator with AI assistance. The AI can suggest wording or movements, but the human operator can accept, change, or reject suggestions and makes the final decisions." AI-only control: "The OriHime here is controlled entirely by an AI system. There is no human operator. The AI system generates OriHime's responses and controls its movements in real time." In the human conditions, a second sentence either said nothing about the operator, stated "The operator of this OriHime has an intellectual disability," or stated "The operator of this OriHime has a mobility-related disability," followed in every human cell by "All operators complete the same training and meet the same standard before they start this work." The AI-only description carried the parallel sentence "All systems are trained and tested to the same standard before they are used for this work." A one-line reminder of the control source appeared above each clip.

### 3.4 Measures

*Pre-disclosure measures.* Before reading the description, participants completed ten items of the Negative Attitudes towards Robots Scale (α = .86) [@nomura2006nars], the short General Attitudes towards Artificial Intelligence Scale (positive subscale, five items, α = .92; negative subscale, four items, α = .85) [@schepman2020gaais], and stereotype-content ratings of people with disabilities (competence, five items, α = .85; warmth, four items, α = .92) [@fiske2002model], together with frequency of contact with people with disabilities. All scales used the same 7-point format as the outcome items.

*Clip-level outcomes.* After each clip, participants rated on 7-point agreement scales three statements about OriHime ("In this interaction, OriHime was trustworthy for this task"; "was useful for this task"; "I would be willing to take part in an interaction like this one with OriHime"), averaged into an *overall evaluation of OriHime* (α = .79) and also analysed separately; one statement of *perceived genuineness* ("This interaction felt genuine, rather than like the execution of a program"); and two statements about the controller named in the description, *warm* and *competent*. In the human conditions a further item asked whether the operator "was in control of what OriHime said and did", which served as evidence that the control-source text was processed. Participants then ranked the actors available in their condition (the human operator where present, the AI system where present, "OriHime or its provider", and the person in the video) by responsibility for a described negative outcome and, separately, by credit for a described positive outcome.

*Participant-level measures.* After the third clip, participants rated performance expectancy (four items, α = .96) and hedonic motivation (three items, α = .97) toward OriHime in general, adapted from UTAUT2 [@venkatesh2012utaut2], then how much they had believed the description of the operator given at the start, and finally demographics. Because belief was measured after all outcomes, it is used only for sensitivity analysis, never for exclusion.

### 3.5 Analysis plan

The analysis sample, the four primary outcomes (overall evaluation of OriHime, perceived genuineness, controller warmth, controller competence), the primary model and the primary contrasts were fixed before any inferential statistic was computed, but after data collection and a descriptive pass, and were not registered. We therefore call these analyses *pre-specified* rather than confirmatory, and label everything else secondary, robustness or exploratory.

Clip-level outcomes were analysed with linear mixed-effects models with fixed effects of condition (seven cells), clip and presentation position and a random intercept for participant, which accounts for the dependence among a participant's three ratings (intraclass correlations .40 to .78). Condition varies between participants and so has no within-participant slope; with three observations per participant we did not estimate clip slopes, a simplification we return to in the limitations [@barr2013random]. Models were fitted by restricted maximum likelihood, omnibus tests used likelihood-ratio tests on maximum-likelihood fits, and contrasts used Wald z tests on equal-weight combinations of the seven cell means: the six human-described cells versus the AI-only cell; the three human-only cells versus the three human-with-AI cells; the four disability-disclosure cells versus the two no-mention cells; and intellectual versus mobility disclosure, which tests the pooling assumed by the third contrast. Primary contrasts are reported with unadjusted p values and with Holm adjustment across the four primary outcomes (α = .05, two-sided). All 21 pairwise differences between conditions were also estimated for every outcome, with Holm and Benjamini–Hochberg adjustment within each outcome's family, and are used to describe the pattern of differences, not to select contrasts (Supplementary Table S1). Effect sizes are model estimates in scale points with 95 percent confidence intervals, and Cohen's d on participant-level means.

Because between-participant variance was larger in the AI-only cell (standard-deviation ratios 1.3 to 1.5; Levene p ≤ .014), every primary contrast was re-estimated under seven alternative specifications: all 300 completers; the analysis sample without 15 straight-lining participants; covariate adjustment for centred pre-disclosure attitudes and age; Welch's t and the Mann–Whitney test on participant means; a participant cluster bootstrap (4,000 resamples); and, for single-item outcomes, proportional-odds GEE models with participant clusters. For contrasts expected to be null we report two one-sided tests (TOST) on participant means with bounds of ±0.35 SD, the smallest standardised difference detectable with 80 percent power for the human-only versus human-with-AI contrast, and ±0.50 SD [@lakens2017equivalence]. These bounds describe the design's sensitivity, not a domain-derived smallest effect of interest, so we report them as bounds on the difference rather than as practical equivalence. For the disclosure contrast we add BIC-approximated Bayes factors on participant-level means [@wagenmakers2007practical].

Whether an actor was ranked first was modelled with generalized estimating equations (binomial, logit link, participant clusters, clip as covariate). Because the human-with-AI condition offered four actors and the others three, we also report a common-anchor measure, whether the actor was ranked ahead of both actors offered in every condition, and summarise full rankings by Plackett–Luce worths estimated by minorisation–maximisation with participant-bootstrap intervals [@plackett1975analysis; @hunter2004mm]. Eight exploratory blocks (113 tests, all listed in Supplementary Table S2) were run after the primary results were known. Analyses used Python 3.12 and statsmodels 0.14; code, data and materials are available (see Data Availability). We report checks first, then the three questions in order.
