## 4 Results

### 4.1 Preliminary checks

Participants believed the description they had been given (M = 5.20, SD = 1.52 on the 7-point scale; 76 percent rated 5 or higher, 17 percent 3 or lower), and belief did not differ between conditions (omnibus p = .451; human-described versus AI-only Δ = −0.28, p = .302). Equal belief does not prove that every participant formed the intended mental model, but it shows that no description was rejected. Among participants told that a human operated OriHime, those also told that AI assisted rated the operator as less in control of what OriHime said and did (6.14 vs 5.73; Δ = 0.40 [0.14, 0.66], d = 0.40, p = .002), so the control-source text was processed. Participants in the disclosure cells spent 5.5 s longer on the description page than those in the no-mention cells (p = .023), consistent with reading the additional sentence, and disclosing a disability did not change perceived operator control (Δ = −0.16, p = .245). Between-participant differences accounted for most of the variance in every clip-level rating (intraclass correlations .40 to .78), and the clips differed strongly from one another (Table 1 gives cell means). Omnibus tests of the seven-level condition factor were significant for perceived genuineness (p = .004), marginal for controller warmth (p = .075) and non-significant for the overall evaluation of OriHime (p = .326) and controller competence (p = .572); the pre-specified contrasts are the planned decomposition of this factor.

### 4.2 How the disclosed control source changes evaluation

Describing the controller as an AI system alone lowered perceived genuineness. Compared with the six human-described conditions, the AI-only description reduced genuineness by 0.81 scale points (95 percent CI 0.35 to 1.27; d = 0.62 [0.26, 0.97]; p < .001, Holm-adjusted p = .002; Table 2, Figure 2). The effect held in all 300 completers, without straight-liners, with adjustment for prior attitudes and age, under Welch's t (p = .017), the Mann–Whitney test (p = .023), the participant cluster bootstrap (p = .011) and an ordinal model (OR = 2.23 [1.17, 4.23], p = .014; Supplementary Table S3). Each of the six human-described cells scored higher than the AI-only cell, and three of these pairwise differences survived Holm correction within the 21-comparison family.

The AI-only description also lowered the warmth attributed to the controller (d = 0.52; human-only and human-with-AI each exceeded AI-only by about half a scale point, p = .006 and .007) and, more weakly, the overall evaluation of OriHime (Δ = 0.36 [0.00, 0.71], d = 0.35, p = .048, Holm p = .097), a difference carried by the usefulness item (Δ = 0.38, p = .036) rather than by trust or willingness to take part. Controller competence did not differ (d = 0.17, p = .330). Because the AI-only cell was more variable, we describe the genuineness effect as robust and the warmth and usefulness effects as suggestive: they survive most but not all of the unequal-variance specifications.

Adding AI assistance to a human operator changed nothing detectable. Human-only and human-with-AI control differed by d = −0.08 (overall evaluation), 0.12 (genuineness), 0.02 (warmth) and −0.17 (competence), none significant, and equivalence tests bounded the differences within ±0.35 SD for the overall evaluation and within ±0.50 SD for the single-item outcomes (Table 4). The pattern across the full pairwise map was the same for all four primary outcomes (Figure 1; Supplementary Table S1): of 84 comparisons, the 10 with unadjusted p < .05, against about four expected by chance, all contrasted a human-described cell with the AI-only cell, and none of the 60 comparisons among the six human-described cells reached p < .05. Clip did not moderate the human-versus-AI contrast (likelihood-ratio p ≥ .13; Figure 3), and within each clip the genuineness difference was significant (p ≤ .047). General acceptance of OriHime measured after the clips (performance expectancy, hedonic motivation) did not differ across conditions (omnibus p = .610 and .395).

### 4.3 How disclosed AI involvement redistributes responsibility and credit

Evaluations of the interaction did not distinguish human-only from human-with-AI control, but attributions did. When a human operated OriHime without AI, participants ranked the operator first for responsibility in 72 percent of clips and first for credit in 71 percent. Adding AI assistance lowered the odds of ranking the operator first (responsibility 55 percent, OR = 0.47 [0.31, 0.73], p = .001; credit 51 percent, OR = 0.42 [0.27, 0.64], p < .001; Table 5). Part of this drop is mechanical, because the human-with-AI condition offered four actors rather than three. Anchored on the two actors offered in every condition, the operator was ranked ahead of both OriHime's provider and the person in the video in 72.3 percent of rankings under human-only control and 60.7 percent under human-with-AI control for responsibility (OR = 0.59 [0.36, 0.93], p = .022), and in 71.2 versus 60.2 percent for credit (OR = 0.61, p = .032). The reallocation is real, but smaller than the raw odds ratios suggest.

The lost share went to the AI. Plackett–Luce worths (Table 6, Figure 4) fell from .68 [.62, .75] for an operator working alone to .45 [.38, .53] for an operator with AI assistance, while the AI received .28 [.25, .32] and the shares of OriHime's provider (.19 to .18) and of the person in the video (.13 to .09) did not rise. When the operator was still ranked first in the human-with-AI condition, the AI was ranked second in 64 to 67 percent of rankings. Blame and credit also parted ways under AI assistance: the operator was ranked first for responsibility more often than for credit (a difference of 4.8 percentage points at the participant level, Wilcoxon p = .024), whereas no such asymmetry appeared under human-only control (1.1 points, p = .536) or for the AI under AI-only control.

With no human present, the AI was ranked first in 57 percent (responsibility) and 60 percent (credit) of clips, with a worth of .55 [.43, .66]; most of the remainder went to OriHime's provider (.30). On the common anchor, however, the AI's standing relative to the provider and the interaction partner was similar with and without a human (52 versus 57 percent for responsibility, OR = 1.24, p = .471; 49 versus 60 percent for credit, OR = 1.60, p = .139). The large raw odds ratio for AI-only control therefore mainly reflects the absence of the operator from the list, not a re-evaluation of the AI itself. Disclosing the operator's disability did not change the odds of the operator being ranked first (responsibility OR = 0.89 [0.57, 1.40], p = .624; credit OR = 0.80 [0.51, 1.25], p = .322).

### 4.4 How disclosing the operator's disability affects judgment and attribution

Disclosing that the operator had a disability and favourable evaluation of the interaction coexisted. Across the four primary outcomes, the four disclosure cells did not differ from the two no-mention cells (all p ≥ .11); the point estimates were small and slightly negative, at most d = −0.24 (Table 2). Equivalence tests excluded differences of half a standard deviation or more in either direction (Table 4), and Bayes factors computed on participant-level means favoured the null over a difference by 3 to 12 to one depending on the outcome (Supplementary Table S8), moderate rather than strong evidence. Intellectual- and mobility-disability disclosures did not differ from each other on any outcome, and the pooled contrast gave the same result. Within the human conditions, operator profile had no main effect on any outcome (likelihood-ratio p ≥ .12) and did not interact with control source (p ≥ .18). Performance expectancy, hedonic motivation and belief in the description were likewise flat across profiles; a marginal hedonic-motivation difference (Δ = −0.40, p = .076) traced to a single cell and disappeared after covariate adjustment.

An exploratory sweep found no moderator. The disclosure contrast did not vary by clip, by participants' reported contact with people with disabilities, by their pre-disclosure stereotype ratings of people with disabilities, or by presentation position in any interpretable way (20 tests; Supplementary Table S2). The warmth–competence gap attributed to the controller did not widen under disclosure, so we found no trace of the paternalising pattern the stereotype literature would predict. Together with the unchanged attribution odds reported above and the dwell-time evidence that the sentence was read, these results identify a setting in which making an operator's disability known carried no observable evaluative or attributional cost. The setting has three features that must travel with the result: the disclosure was one written sentence, it was immediately followed by an assurance of equal training, and the clips showed fluent, competent work. Section 5.3 discusses what this does and does not license.

### 4.5 Whose response to AI-only control is most negative (exploratory)

Prior attitude to AI, measured before the description, moderated the human-versus-AI contrast. Ratings rose more steeply with positive attitude in the AI-only condition than in the human-described conditions (slopes per scale point .50 vs .28 for the overall evaluation, interaction p = .019; .38 vs .10 for competence, p = .003; .48 vs .23 for genuineness, p = .083; .28 vs .10 for warmth, p = .093; Figure 5). Among participants one standard deviation below the mean attitude, the AI-only penalty was 0.44 (overall evaluation, p = .014), 0.91 (genuineness, p < .001), 0.62 (warmth, p = .003) and 0.42 scale points (competence, p = .015); among participants one standard deviation above the mean, no difference was significant. The human-with-AI slope (.30) matched the human-only slope (.26): attitude-dependence was triggered by the absence of a human, not by the presence of AI. This pattern had been noticed in a descriptive pass before the formal analysis and is reported as post hoc. A second hint, a competence penalty confined to the relational clip, did not reach significance within that clip (OR = 1.78 [0.88, 3.58], p = .108). Of the 113 exploratory tests, 37 reached p < .05; excluding 11 equivalence tests and 13 mean-rank comparisons that are not comparable across conditions, 13 of the remaining 79 did, against about four expected by chance, and they cluster in the attitude-moderation block and in sample restrictions that reproduce the primary contrast. Restricting the analysis to participants who believed the description (n = 206) left the primary contrast unchanged.

## 5 Discussion

The experiment asked what a single sentence about who controls an avatar robot does to observers who all see the same competent behaviour. The answer has two parts that do not move together. Whether a human was described as present changed how genuine and warm the interaction felt; whether that human had AI assistance, and who that human was, did not. Yet describing AI as involved moved responsibility and credit away from the operator even though evaluations had not moved.

### 5.1 Human presence matters more than operator profile

The AI-only description lowered perceived genuineness by more than half a standard deviation and lowered the warmth attributed to the controller, while every description that kept a human in the loop avoided this cost. The result extends the disclosure effects documented for chatbots, cooperative bots and AI-written text [@luo2019machines; @ishowooloko2019transparency; @jakesch2019aimc; @karinshak2023persuade] to a physically embodied robot whose behaviour was identical across conditions, and it is consistent with the field observation that unrecognised teleoperation is rated better [@baba2020teleoperated]. The three-level design adds what the two-level literature could not show. Acknowledging AI assistance did not cost anything detectable: human-only and human-with-AI control were bounded within a third of a standard deviation on the overall evaluation and within half on the single items. In mind-perception terms [@gray2007dimensions], observers appear to grant the interaction a human mind as long as a human is named as deciding, whatever tools that human uses. Nor did the kind of human matter: an operator described as having an intellectual or a mobility-related disability was judged like an operator not so described. Presence, not profile, did the work.

### 5.2 Responsibility and credit migrate before perception does

Observers who judged human-with-AI interaction exactly as they judged human-only interaction nonetheless ranked the operator first for responsibility and credit less often, and the share they took from the operator went to the AI rather than to the robot's provider or the interaction partner. This is the moral crumple zone seen from the observer's side [@elish2019moral]. Hohenstein and Jung found that an AI in the conversation absorbed blame when things went wrong [@hohenstein2020crumple]; here the AI also absorbed credit when things went right, and the asymmetry ran against the human. Under AI assistance the operator kept more of the responsibility than of the credit, which echoes the finding that in shared control the human is blamed more when both parties err [@awad2020drivers] and the finding that agentic framing of an AI pulls credit toward it [@epstein2020credit]. The practical reading is uncomfortable for avatar work. A system can add AI assistance without any visible loss in how the interaction is received, and precisely for that reason the operator's diminished recognition will not show up in customer satisfaction. Recognition has to be designed for directly. Two features of the data sharpen the point. First, the reallocation survived the common-anchor correction, so it is not an artefact of a longer list of actors: even against the two actors present in every condition, an AI-assisted operator was ranked ahead less often. Second, the AI's own standing barely changed between the AI-assisted and AI-only descriptions on that anchor. Observers did not credit the AI more once the human was gone; they simply had no human to credit. The operator, in other words, is the variable, and what is at stake is the visibility of their labour rather than a re-evaluation of the machine.

### 5.3 Disability disclosure without an observable penalty

The encouraging finding is that disclosing an operator's disability and a favourable evaluation of their work coexisted. Observers told that the operator had an intellectual or a mobility-related disability rated the interaction, the controller's warmth and competence, and the operator's responsibility and credit no differently, within bounds of half a standard deviation and with Bayes factors moderately favouring the null. The stereotype literature predicts a competence penalty in work contexts [@fiske2002model; @rohmer2018implicit]; we did not observe one, and the warmth–competence gap did not widen. For a workforce whose employment runs through the robot, this identifies a concrete setting in which an operator could make their disability known without being judged less favourably, and it supports designing avatar-mediated work so that disclosure is the operator's choice rather than something the system must hide.

The result is bounded in four ways, and the bounds are part of the finding. The disclosure was a single written sentence, never repeated or depicted. It was followed at once by an assurance that all operators complete the same training, which directly answers the competence stereotype and may have neutralised it; whether the result survives without that sentence is unknown. The clips showed fluent, competent performance, so the label competed with strong behavioural evidence, and prior work on contact suggests that observing competent performance may itself reduce prejudice [@pettigrew2006meta]. And the data remain compatible with small negative effects: the point estimates were slightly negative, the design had 41 percent power for an effect of d = −0.24, and the control condition said nothing about disability rather than stating its absence, so the contrast is explicit disclosure versus omission. The study therefore does not show that stigma is absent, that disclosure never matters, or that workplaces are already inclusive. It shows that under these conditions disclosure carried no observable cost, which is the claim the design can support.

[CONDITIONAL, baseline-dependent: if a fresh cohort shown the same clips without any description mostly infers that the operator has a disability, the claim weakens to "explicit disclosure added no detectable incremental effect"; if they mostly cannot tell, the label supplied information the clips did not, and the bounded null stands in its stronger form. Do not draft before data exist.]

### 5.4 Who is most affected by an AI-only description?

In an exploratory analysis, the AI-only penalty was concentrated among participants with less positive prior attitudes to AI; among the most positive, no penalty was detectable. The human-with-AI slope matched the human-only slope, so what triggered attitude-dependence was the absence of a human, not the presence of AI. The pattern is consistent with algorithm aversion being a disposition that some people bring to the encounter rather than a uniform response [@dietvorst2015algorithm; @schepman2020gaais]. It was noticed before the formal analysis, it is one of 113 exploratory tests, and it needs replication before it can guide practice.

### 5.6 Design implications

*Keep the human visible in the disclosure.* Wording that names the person and then the assistance ("operated by a team member, with AI assistance") preserved genuineness and warmth in our data, whereas wording that named only the AI cost both. This follows directly from the pre-specified control-source contrasts (strong evidence). Guidelines for human–AI interaction already ask systems to make clear what they can do [@amershi2019guidelines]; for avatar work the guideline should add: make clear who is doing it.

*Design recognition, not just disclosure.* Because observers reassigned credit to the AI without any change in how they evaluated the interaction, satisfaction metrics will not reveal the loss. Operator identity on or beside the robot, contribution records that distinguish operator decisions from AI suggestions, and credit lines in service records are ways to make the operator's contribution legible (strong evidence for the problem; the remedies are proposals).

*Make disability disclosure the operator's choice.* We observed no cost of disclosure and no benefit either. Systems and employers should neither require operators to disclose nor prevent them, and should keep the equal-standards framing that accompanied disclosure here (moderate evidence, bounded as in §5.3).

*Expect audience differences.* If the exploratory moderation replicates, disclosure may matter most for AI-sceptical audiences, and disclosure design might be tested against that group specifically (weak evidence; a research direction).

*Report what a null rules out.* Human–robot interaction studies that find no effect of a label or a profile should report equivalence bounds and Bayes factors so that readers can tell an inconclusive result from one that excludes effects of a stated size [@lakens2017equivalence; @wagenmakers2007practical].

## 6 Limitations

The design allocated one cell to AI-only control and six to human control, so contrasts involving AI-only rest on 36 participants against 236 and could detect a difference of about d = 0.50 with 80 percent power; smaller differences, for example in competence, cannot be excluded. Between-participant variance was also larger in that cell, which is why the warmth and usefulness effects are called suggestive. The genuineness item ("felt genuine, rather than like the execution of a program") is worded close to the AI-only description, so part of its effect may reflect consistency with the text; the warmth, usefulness and attribution effects, whose wording is not tied to the manipulation, argue against a purely demand-driven account but do not remove the concern. There was no direct manipulation check of control source; equal belief across conditions, the operator-control item and the attribution results show that the text was read and believed, not that it produced the intended model in every participant.

The disability disclosure was minimal, and the material worked against it: participants had just rated stereotype items about people with disabilities in every cell, the disclosure was followed by an equal-training assurance, and the clips showed competent work. Uptake of the disability sentence specifically was not measured beyond dwell time. The null applies to this textual form of disclosure in this framing and should not be generalised to visible or repeated cues. Rankings concerned hypothetical outcomes rather than real consequences. The three clips confound task type with length and content, so clip effects are treated as nuisance terms. Single-item outcomes had ceiling effects (24 to 36 percent at the maximum), which compress differences among the human conditions. Participants were an English-speaking online panel, mostly in the United States, judging one Japanese robot in one workplace, so the results speak to how such a deployment would be received by observers of that kind. The mixed models used a random intercept only; the analyses on participant means agree with them. The analysis plan was fixed before inference but after a descriptive pass and was not preregistered, so we call the main analyses pre-specified rather than confirmatory; all 113 exploratory tests are reported. Finally, the independent no-disclosure baseline is a separate cohort; comparisons between it and this experiment are descriptive and cannot replicate the causal disclosure contrasts. [If baseline estimates are inconclusive, say so here rather than using them to support the headline.] Two further limits concern the model and the measures. The mixed models used a participant random intercept but no clip slopes, which can understate uncertainty for within-participant effects [@barr2013random]; our inferences concern between-participant contrasts, and the participant-mean analyses, which make no such assumption, agree. The responsibility and credit items asked participants to rank actors for a described negative and positive outcome rather than to allocate real consequences, and the actor list differed by condition; the common-anchor measure and the Plackett–Luce worths address the second problem but not the first.


## 7 Conclusion

A sentence about who controls an avatar robot changed how an identical interaction was judged, but only through one thing: whether a human was said to be present. Naming a human kept the interaction genuine and the controller warm; adding AI assistance cost nothing perceptible; disclosing that the operator had an intellectual or a mobility-related disability changed neither evaluations nor attributions within the bounds the design could set. At the same time, naming AI as involved moved responsibility and credit away from the operator while evaluations stood still. For the people whose route into work runs through a robot, the design lesson is to keep the human named, credited and free to decide what to disclose. Bringing the human back is not only a matter of how the robot behaves; it is a matter of what we say about who is behind it.
