## Section plan

### 0 Abstract (≤150 words) + CCS concepts + keywords
- **Content**: context and importance (avatar robots expand access to work for people with disabilities, while AI assistance can make the human contribution harder to see); question (does a one-line disclosure change judgments of an identical interaction); design (N = 300, 7 descriptions × 3 clips); and three fully named findings with effect sizes: the evaluation cost of an AI-only description relative to descriptions that retain human presence; the redistribution of responsibility and credit when AI is involved; and the encouraging but bounded finding that disability disclosure and favourable evaluation coexisted without a detectable evaluation or attribution penalty in this setting. The exploratory moderation by prior AI attitudes and the pending no-disclosure baseline do not enter the abstract.
- **Sources**: none.

### 1 Introduction (≈900 words)
- **1.1 Why avatar-mediated work matters (≈350)** — Purpose: establish both the deployment reality and its human stakes. Content: telepresence can let people work where their bodies cannot easily go; OriHime and avatar work in Japan (DAWN café, hospitals, offices) have supported employment, social participation, fulfilment and identity presentation for operators with disabilities; AI assistance through speech support, shared autonomy and generated responses is entering the same loop. The audience may therefore need a written description to know whether the observed behaviour is human work, human–AI work or AI performance. Explain why this matters: the description may determine whether the human is visible, who receives recognition, and whether the operator can disclose a disability without being judged less favourably. Sources: T1, T2. Transition: "The people who meet an avatar robot therefore judge not only what it does, but who they believe is acting through it."
- **1.2 What is known, what is missing and why the questions belong together (≈300)** — Purpose: motivate the integrated problem without presenting the experiment as seven arbitrary conditions. Establish four prior conclusions: avatar work can benefit operators; AI labels can change evaluation; automation can redistribute blame and credit; and disability disclosure can activate workplace stereotypes. Then name the missing link: these traditions do not show how the three processes operate together when observers see identical competent behaviour. Introduce the three consequences of a disclosure sentence—control-source evaluation, allocation of responsibility and credit, and identity-related judgment—and the practical stakes for operator agency, recognition and inclusion. Sources: T1–T4 (one nearest empirical anchor per claim). Transition to the four fully worded research questions.
- **1.3 Integrated gap, research questions and contributions (≈250)** — Scope novelty to the documented 83-record search corpus. State the corpus-level gap in full: no randomised study of a physically embodied avatar robot holds behaviour constant while comparing human-only, human-with-AI and AI-only descriptions, jointly maps responsibility and credit across the operator, AI, provider and interaction partner, and tests intellectual- and mobility-disability disclosure. Then state the four questions in full: how disclosed control source changes evaluation; how AI involvement redistributes responsibility and credit; how disability disclosure changes judgment and attribution; and, exploratorily, whose response to an AI-only description is most negative. Give four contribution bullets from `stories/story_unified.md`; keep the exploratory question in the body but outside the abstract and do not promise the pending baseline as a contribution before results. Transition: "We first review what each literature already establishes, then show the missing connection among them."

### 2 Related Work (≈1,300 words)
- **2.1 Avatar robots and avatar work (≈300)** — **Known:** telepresence robots can support remote participation; OriHime field and qualitative studies document accessible work, fulfilment, identity negotiation and choices about what operators reveal. **Missing:** this evidence is predominantly operator-side, qualitative and small (n = 7–10); the 83-record corpus contains no large controlled third-party evaluation of a disabled operator's robot-mediated work. **Why it matters:** access to a workplace does not guarantee that customers and coworkers will recognise or evaluate the operator's contribution fairly. Sources: T1 (`takeuchi2020avatar`; `hatada2024redefining`; `zhang2022telepresence`).
- **2.2 Disclosing automation and the perception of an interaction (≈350)** — **Known:** Wizard-of-Oz practice and mind-perception work show that observers infer agency; bot, voice and text studies show that AI disclosure or suspected AI authorship can lower purchasing, trust or cooperation. The closest robot field study associates unrecognised teleoperation with better service ratings. **Missing:** prior work is mostly text/voice, mainly contrasts human with AI, and the closest robot result did not randomise realisation or fix behaviour. No study in the corpus holds embodied avatar behaviour constant while varying human-only, human-with-AI and AI-only descriptions. **Why it matters:** the social cost may come from removing the human from the description rather than from acknowledging AI assistance itself. Sources: T2 (`baba2020teleoperated`; `luo2019machines`; `jakesch2019aimc`; `riek2012wizard`).
- **2.3 Responsibility and credit in human–AI teams (≈300)** — **Known:** moral-crumple-zone accounts and studies of robots, AI-mediated chat, shared-control driving and AI-generated art show that perceived autonomy and framing redistribute attribution. **Missing:** credit is less represented than blame, and no screened study jointly ranks responsibility and credit across the operator, AI, robot provider and interaction partner in AI-assisted avatar work. **Why it matters:** an interaction may remain warmly evaluated while the human loses recognition for their labour or retains disproportionate blame. Sources: T3 (`elish2019moral`; `hohenstein2020crumple`; `kim2006blame`; `epstein2020credit`).
- **2.4 Disability disclosure, stigma and stereotype content (≈300)** — **Known:** disability can evoke paternalising or work-specific warmth–competence stereotypes; disclosure strategy and attributed onset can affect hiring intentions; contact can reduce prejudice. **Missing:** no large controlled perceiver-side study in the corpus fixes visibly competent avatar behaviour while varying intellectual- and mobility-disability disclosure. Relevant HRI and disclosure nulls also rarely use equivalence tests or Bayes factors to bound what the data rule out. **Why it matters:** operators should be able to control self-presentation without an avoidable evaluation or attribution cost, but positive evidence must remain conditional on the material and social setting. Sources: T4 (`fiske2002model`; `rohmer2018implicit`; `lyons2017disclosing`; `pettigrew2006meta`).
- **2.5 Integrated gap (≈50)** — Synthesize rather than repeat: prior work separately addresses operator experience, reactions to AI labels, responsibility or credit, and disability stigma. Within the documented 83-record corpus, none connects all four in one randomised identical-behaviour avatar-robot experiment. Transition: "The experiment addresses evaluation, attribution and disability disclosure in one controlled design."

### 3 Method (≈1,350 words)
- **3.1 Design (≈200)** — 3 × 2 + 1 between-subjects: human-only control and human control with AI assistance were each paired with no disability mention, intellectual-disability disclosure or mobility-disability disclosure, plus one AI-only condition; three clips within participants, six orders, server-side randomisation to 42 cells. Sources: `methods.md`.
- **3.2 Participants (≈200)** — CloudResearch Connect; N = 300; exclusions (AT1 21, AV1 8, both 1) → n = 272; demographics (Table 1 header row; numbers sheet). Ethics statement pointer.
- **3.3 Materials (≈250)** — OriHime; three clips (REL 70 s, ADV 105 s, COL 115 s) with identical footage across conditions; the seven descriptions verbatim (supplementary), including the equal-training sentence; disclosure page.
- **3.4 Measures (≈300)** — OH1–3 composite (α .79), AU1 genuineness, CR1 warmth, CR2 competence, CR3 (manipulation evidence), BEL1 credibility; responsibility and credit rankings and actor sets (3 vs 4 actors); GAAIS (α .92/.85), NARS (.86), SCM (.85/.92), contact, demographics; item wording in supplementary.
- **3.5 Analysis plan (≈350)** — pre-specified before inference, not preregistered; LMM (REML) with participant random intercept, clip and position fixed; omnibus LRTs (ML); equal-weight cell contrasts C1–C5; Holm within families; TOST bounds at the design MDE (±.35 SD OH, ±.50 others) and why they are MDEs, not SESOIs; participant-unit BIC Bayes factors; cluster bootstrap after Levene; GEE logistic and Plackett–Luce for rankings; common-anchor measure; exploratory bookkeeping (113 tests). Software: Python 3.12, statsmodels; code and data availability. Sources: T5 (LMM, TOST, BF, PL, scales). Transition: "We report checks first, then the three questions in order."

### 4 Results (≈1,900 words, including any triggered baseline insert)
- **4.1 Checks (≈200)** — exclusions; BEL1 equal across conditions (p .451, M 5.20); CR3 H > HA (d .40, p .002) as evidence the control description was read; disclosure page dwell +5.5 s; ICCs .40–.78 justify the multilevel model. Evidence: numbers sheet; Table 1.
- **4.2 How disclosed control source changes evaluation (≈550)** — omnibus (genuineness p .004; warmth .075; overall evaluation .326; competence .572); human-described versus AI-only control (genuineness Δ .81, d .62, Holm .002; warmth d .52; overall evaluation via usefulness d .38; competence n.s.); robustness list (Table 3); human-only versus human-with-AI bounded within the equivalence intervals (Table 4); pairwise pattern rule 10/84, all human-described cells above AI-only, 0/60 among human-described cells (Figure 1); Holm survivors named; clip does not moderate (Figure 3); heteroscedastic AI-only cell, bootstrap agrees. Evidence: Tables 2–4, Figures 1–3. Tags: [pre-specified].
- **4.3 How AI involvement redistributes responsibility and credit (≈450)** — operator ranked first 72 %/71 % under human-only control and 55 %/51 % under human-with-AI control (odds ratios .47/.42); common anchor 72.3→60.7 %, 71.2→60.2 % (odds ratios .59/.61); Plackett–Luce worths .68→.45 for the operator, AI .28, provider and interlocutor flat (Figure 4, Table 6); blame exceeds credit under human-with-AI control (+4.8 points, p .024); under AI-only control, AI is first 57 %/60 %, but the common-anchor comparison with human-with-AI is not significant, so the raw odds ratio mainly reflects the missing human; disability disclosure does not change attribution (odds ratios .89/.80). Evidence: Tables 5–7, Figure 4. Tags: [pre-specified], [added after cross-review] for Table 7.
- **4.4 How disability disclosure changes judgment and attribution (≈350)** — lead with the encouraging result that disability disclosure and favourable evaluation coexisted under the conditions tested. Disclosed disability versus no mention is nonsignificant on all four primary outcomes (p ≥ .11), with small negative estimates up to d = −.24; equivalence tests exclude |d| ≥ .50; participant-unit Bayes factors range from 3 to 12 (Table 8); intellectual- and mobility-disability disclosures do not differ and the pooled result is the same; the contextual-variable sweep is null (clip, prior disability contact, stereotype content and position; 20 tests); uptake evidence; post-exposure and credibility measures are flat by operator profile. Close with the positive inclusion-related implication and its limits using the five disability-disclosure rules in `story_unified.md`. Evidence: Tables 2, 4, 8. Tags: [pre-specified] for the planned contrasts; [exploratory] for the sweep.
- **4.5 Whose response to AI-only control is most negative (exploratory; ≈150)** — prior AI-attitude moderation (overall-evaluation slopes .50 vs .28, interaction p .019; competence p .003; simple slopes at ±1 SD; Figure 5); usefulness carries the overall-evaluation difference; casual-conversation competence remains a weak lead (odds ratio 1.78, p .108); "113 exploratory tests, 37 at p < .05, none promoted; all listed in Supplementary Table S2". This subsection remains outside the abstract. Tags: [exploratory].
- **4.6 Independent no-disclosure baseline (0 or ≈250–400)** — not automatically present. Full methods/results always go in supplementary material. Add this concise evidence subsection only if a clear WHO pattern, meaningful clip heterogeneity or a consequential DIS pattern changes the interpretation of §§4.2 or 4.4. Follow `no_disclosure_baseline_integration.md`; never label it Study 2, replication or robustness test. If no trigger is met, omit §4.6 and use at most 1–3 contextualising sentences or a Limitations note.

### 5 Discussion (≈1,850 words, including Design Implications)
- **5.1 Human presence matters more than operator profile (≈300)** — the human label buys genuineness and warmth; AI assistance costs nothing perceptible; who the human is changes nothing detectable. If baseline results exist, distinguish an AI-label cost, a human-presence benefit, asymmetric deviation from a human-with-AI default, an informational blank or clip-specific defaults. Ties to the control-source-disclosure and avatar-work literatures.
- **5.2 Responsibility and credit migrate before perception does (≈300)** — human-with-AI control was evaluated like human-only control but attributed more like AI-only control; blame sticks to the human more than credit does; moral crumple zone read from the observer's side. Ties to the responsibility, blame and credit literature.
- **5.3 Disability disclosure without an observable penalty (≈300)** — lead with the encouraging inclusion-related implication: avatar-mediated work may provide a setting in which operators with disabilities can disclose their disability without being judged less favourably. Explain that the data identify a condition in which disclosure and favourable evaluation coexist, then state the boundaries (one-line text, competent behaviour and equal-training assurance) and clarify that the study does not demonstrate the absence of stigma or an already-inclusive workplace. If baseline results exist, distinguish a no-disability default, a disability default, uncertainty, heterogeneity and dependence on perceived control source. A disability default forces the weaker "no incremental effect of explicit disclosure" wording. Ties to the disability-disclosure and stigma literature.
- **5.4 Who is most affected by an AI-only description? (≈200)** — exploratory; participants with less positive AI attitudes; consistent with attitude-dependent algorithm aversion; needs replication. This result stays outside the abstract.
- **5.5 Default impressions as an interpretive boundary (0–200)** — conditional synthesis only; do not restate the supplementary baseline report or imply causal cross-cohort comparison.
- **5.6 Design implications (≈450)** — human-visible disclosure wording; credit protocols for assisted work; disability disclosure as the operator's choice; audience-aware disclosure (tentative); bounded-null reporting. Each implication states what to do, which finding supports it and its evidence strength. Sources: T6.

### 6 Limitations (≈500 words)
Single-sentence manipulations and no disability-specific belief check; A cell n = 36 with larger variance; fixed, non-neutral clips; hypothetical outcomes for attribution; US/CA sample judging a Japanese deployment; BEL1 post-treatment; not preregistered; the independent baseline is a separate cohort and cannot replicate the causal disclosure contrast; 41 % power at d = .24 for C3. If baseline estimates are inconclusive, state that explicitly rather than using them to support the headline.
