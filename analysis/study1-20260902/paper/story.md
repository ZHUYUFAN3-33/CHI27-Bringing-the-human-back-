# Trial storyline — "Bringing the Human Back?"

> **STATUS: TRIAL.** Revised 2026-09-04 after an independent cross-review (`analysis/study1-20260902-cross-review/`): "confirmatory" is now "pre-specified (not preregistered)", equivalence claims are stated as bounds, and the disability result is a bounded null rather than evidence of no effect. This storyline was chosen on 2026-09-04 as a working frame for the Methods/Results drafts. It is not a decision. Every claim below is tagged with its evidence level; the tags must survive into the manuscript.

## One-sentence thesis

Telling people that a human is behind the robot changes how genuine, warm and useful the interaction feels — but only the *presence* of a human matters, not whether AI assists them and not who the human is; what AI involvement does change is who gets the blame and the credit.

## The arc

**Act 1 — the setup (Methods).** All participants watched the same three clips. The only thing that varied was one page of text: who controls OriHime (human / human with AI assistance / AI only) and, when a human was present, whether the operator was described as having an intellectual disability, a mobility disability, or not described at all. Any difference between conditions is therefore a difference in prior attribution, not in observed behaviour.

**Act 2 — what human presence changes (pre-specified contrast C1).** Compared with the six human-involved cells, the AI-only description made the interaction feel less genuine (d = .62, robust to every specification), the controller less warm (d = .52, suggestive: depends on the equal-variance assumption) and OriHime less useful and less worth taking part in (suggestive), while judgements of competence were unchanged. Trustworthiness itself did not differ. *Allowed:* "reduced perceived genuineness, warmth and usefulness." *Not allowed:* "reduced trust."

**Act 3 — what does not change (pre-specified contrasts C2 and C3, reported as bounds).** Differences between human and human-with-AI operation were bounded within ±0.35 SD for OriHime evaluation, genuineness, warmth and hedonic motivation, and within ±0.50 SD for every measure (TOST; the bounds are this design's smallest detectable effect, not a domain-derived smallest effect of interest). Explicitly disclosing that the operator has an intellectual or mobility disability, compared with not mentioning disability, produced no detectable average difference in evaluations or attributions; all point estimates were small and negative (genuineness and warmth d ≈ −.24 with intervals reaching −.5), effects larger than about half an SD are excluded, and no moderator was found among clip, contact frequency, or stereotype content. This is a bounded null for one-sentence explicit disclosure in this material, not evidence that disability status has no effect. The one measure that did respond to the AI-assistance text — perceived operator control — shows the text was read (d = .40). *Allowed:* "differences bounded within ±0.35/±0.50 SD"; "no detectable average effect of explicit disclosure; effects larger than about half an SD excluded." *Not allowed:* "identical"; "equivalent" without stating the bound and its origin; "disability has no effect"; "proves absence of bias."

**Act 4 — the cost lands on attribution (pre-specified, RQ4).** Adding AI assistance lowered the odds that the human operator was held most responsible (72% → 55%, OR .47) or given most credit (71% → 51%, OR .42). Because the human+AI condition offered one more actor to rank, part of that drop is mechanical; anchored on the two actors offered in every condition, the operator was ranked ahead of both the provider and the interlocutor in 72% vs 61% of rankings (OR .59 and .61), so the reallocation is real but smaller than the raw odds ratios suggest. The lost share went almost entirely to the AI (Plackett–Luce worth .28), not to the provider or the interlocutor. With no human present, the AI was ranked first in 57–60% of rankings; on the common anchor its standing relative to the provider and the interlocutor was similar with and without a human (52% vs 57%, 49% vs 60%, n.s.), so this mostly reflects the operator's absence from the list. In the human-with-AI condition the operator was slightly more likely to be blamed than credited (OR 1.21, small).

**Coda — who pays the AI-only penalty (exploratory, E1).** The penalty for "AI only" was concentrated among participants with less positive prior attitudes to AI (interaction with GAAIS: p = .019 for OriHime evaluation, .003 for competence; same direction on genuineness and warmth). Among AI-positive participants the two groups did not differ. The human-with-AI slope equalled the human slope: only the *absence* of a human triggers attitude-dependence. *Allowed:* "concentrated among AI-sceptical participants (exploratory)." *Not allowed:* "AI-positive participants preferred the AI-controlled robot."

## Stories the data cannot support

- "AI-controlled robots are trusted less" — trustworthiness did not differ (p = .10–.17 across methods).
- "Disclosing a disability changes how the operator is judged" — not detected here; effects beyond ~0.5 SD excluded, smaller negative effects not.
- "Disability status has no effect on how the operator is judged" — also not supported: the study tested one explicit sentence, preceded by stereotype items and followed by an equal-training assurance and competent behaviour.
- "AI assistance undermines the human operator's standing" — perceptions were equivalent; only attribution shifted.
- "The disability effect appears in specific tasks or people" — 20 moderator tests, none significant.
- "People who like AI prefer the AI-controlled robot" — the reversed difference at +1 SD is not significant.
- "Position of the clip matters for competence" — the position × condition interaction decomposes into nothing.
