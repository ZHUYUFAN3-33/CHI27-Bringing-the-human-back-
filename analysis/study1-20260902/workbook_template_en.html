Phase 1.5 · Design reconstruction

Study design

Everything below was reconstructed from the questionnaire code (shared/instrument.js) and the export; please confirm each point.

Participants and allocation

English-speaking participants recruited on CloudResearch Connect: 263 in the United States, 30 in Canada, 6 in the United Kingdom, 1 in Ireland. Ages 18–78 (mean [[AGE_MEAN]]), half women. Each person took part once; the condition was assigned by the server: 42 cells (7 conditions × 6 clip orders), each newcomer placed in the currently least-filled cell, ties broken at random. Condition sizes are therefore almost equal (41–45) and clip order is balanced within every condition.

Manipulation

Before any clip, participants read a page “about the OriHime you will see”. The clips themselves were identical for everyone; the only difference between conditions is that this text told them who controls OriHime and, in the human conditions, who the operator is. Any condition difference can therefore only come from this prior description, not from inference about the robot's behaviour — this is the core of the design and the point the interpretation keeps returning to.

  • Factor 1 · control source ctrl (between subjects): H controlled in real time by a trained person, no AI at all; HA a person controls, an AI suggests wording and movements, the person makes the final decisions; A controlled entirely by an AI system, no human.
  • Factor 2 · operator profile profile (between subjects, exists only when there is a human): 1 = nothing said (control); 2 = “the operator has an intellectual disability”; 3 = “the operator has a mobility-related disability”. Condition A has no operator, so this factor does not apply — the design is 3 × 2 plus one reference cell, not a full 3 × 3.
  • Factor 3 · clip / task type clip (within subjects): everyone watched three clips: REL, a chat about recent life (70 s); ADV, advice on workload (105 s); COL, collaboratively editing and printing a document (115 s). All six orders were used, so presentation position (1–3) and the preceding clip are balanced.
  • Shape of the sample: H 128, HA 128, but A only 44 (one cell, one seventh of the sample). Every “human vs no human” comparison is 256 against 44 (236 against 36 in the primary sample), which fixes the precision of that contrast, discussed later.

Measurement order

  1. Consent and eligibility (E1–E3).
  2. Pre-measures: negative attitudes towards robots NARS (10 items), general attitudes to AI GAAIS (9 items), stereotype content about people with disabilities SCM (9 items). All answered before the manipulation, so they are clean baseline variables.
  3. Manipulation: the condition description page (control source + profile + a diagram); click “I have read it”.
  4. Clip 1 → comprehension check AV1 (first clip only) → OH1 trustworthy, OH2 useful, OH3 willing to take part, AU1 felt genuine → CR1 controller warm, CR2 controller competent, CR3 operator in control (not asked in A) → R1 who bears most responsibility for a negative outcome (ranking), R2 who deserves most credit for a positive outcome (ranking).
  5. Clip 2: as above, plus the attention check AT1 (“please select Disagree”).
  6. Clip 3: as above.
  7. General items: PE1–4 OriHime would be useful in daily life, HM1–3 conversing with OriHime seems fun.
  8. BEL1: “how much did you believe the description of the operator given at the beginning”, then demographics and contact frequencies.
  9. Debrief and willingness to be contacted for a follow-up interview, FU1.

One fact you must know: the mockup designed two manipulation checks (C1 “who controls”, C2 “operator profile”), but the v6 instrument does not contain them; check_c1_pass / check_c2_pass are empty in the export. Only two things in the data can play a similar role: BEL1 (belief asked at the end, subjective and after the fact) and CR3 (“the operator was in control of what OriHime said and did”, asked only in H/HA; its H–HA difference is exactly what the manipulation should produce). The paper therefore cannot say “manipulation check passed”; it can only report the BEL1 distribution and the H–HA difference on CR3 as indirect evidence.

Unit of analysis

The data have three levels, which must be separated before choosing a method:

  • Participant level (300 rows, one per person): PE, HM, BEL1, the pre-measures, demographics. One number per person, independent of each other (different people).
  • Participant × clip level (900 rows, three per person): OH1–3, AU1, CR1–3, R1/R2. A person's three rows are not independent: someone who likes robots rates all three clips high. Treating 900 rows as 900 independent people understates the standard errors and makes p-values too small — this is the fundamental reason for a mixed model rather than a plain t-test or ANOVA.
  • Participant × clip × actor level (rankings): each ranking gives 3 or 4 actors a rank, and the ranks constrain each other (if one is first, no other can be). This needs dedicated methods (first the share ranked first, then paired or ranking models).

One more point: clip is a within-subject factor (everyone sees all three), control source and profile are between-subject factors (each person is in one condition). This is a mixed design, with clip-level observations nested in participants.

Scales and reliability

Principle: for a multi-item scale, first check whether the items “say the same thing” (internal consistency), then decide whether they may be averaged; a single Likert item stays a single item and is never silently treated as continuous for convenience. α below is Cronbach's alpha (see the concepts section); ≥ .70 is usually taken as acceptable for a composite.

[[T_ALPHA]]
  • The OH composite can be formed (α ≈ .80; .78–.82 within each clip). But OH3 “willing to take part” has a clearly lower mean and a wider spread than OH1/OH2, so the main tables keep the three items as well.
  • CR1 and CR2 correlate r = .67 and could be combined, but they map onto the theoretically distinct “warmth” and “competence” dimensions (as in the SCM pre-measure), so reporting them separately carries more information. CR3 correlates only .34 with CR2 — it measures something else (control) and is not merged.
  • GAAIS: the two subscales correlate .72 and are reported separately as the authors recommend; NARS used only 10 of the 14 items (four attitude-manufacturing items were dropped), which the paper must state as a reduced form. NARS_07 (“the word robot means nothing to me”) barely correlates with the total (.13) and pulls the S1 subscale α down to .70.

Below are the distributions of the clip-level single items. Note the ceiling effects: 36% of CR3 answers are 7, and more than a quarter of CR2 and OH2 answers are 7. Scores piled at the top compress condition differences (there is no room to go higher) and skew the distributions to the left, one reason ordinal models are used later as a robustness check.

Phase 3 · Data quality

Data quality checks

  • Completeness: 300 completers, no duplicate external_pid; no out-of-range Likert values; ages 18–78; all 1,800 rankings are valid permutations; exactly one AV1 (first clip) and one AT1 (second clip) per person; missingness only by design (CR3 not asked in A, no AI row in H, no operator row in A) plus one missing age.
  • Video compliance: all 900 clips opened the answer gate by playing to the end (gate_open); nobody passed by dragging the scrubber; 2 people used the fallback player for one clip; with one exception every page dwell was ≥ the clip length. The player reported 7 errors for 4 people, all recovered.
  • Attention check AT1 (instructed to select “Disagree”): [[N_AT1]] / 300 passed. Of the 21 failures, [[N_AT1_NEAR]] chose the adjacent “Strongly disagree” — they read the instruction but not the exact option; the other 10 chose 3–7, more like not reading. Condition A had the most failures (6). Mobile users make up 38% of the failures but only 11% overall.
  • Comprehension check AV1 (what happened in the first clip): [[N_AV1]] / 300 passed. With REL first 99/99 were correct, ADV 99/101, COL 94/100 — the COL item (“the air conditioning was set to twenty-two degrees”) is slightly harder. [[N_BOTH]] people passed both checks.
  • Response style: [[N_SL3]] people gave the same score to every item on all three clip pages (mostly all 7 or all 6); most of them passed both checks. They are not necessarily careless (someone who genuinely thinks everything is fine would answer this way), but they carry no information about condition differences, so a “does anything change without them” sensitivity analysis is worthwhile.
  • Duration: median [[DUR_MED]] min (IQR [[DUR_Q1]]–[[DUR_Q3]]), fastest [[DUR_MIN]], slowest [[DUR_MAX]]. The three clips add up to almost 5 minutes, so 8.5 minutes is still a plausible lower bound; nobody was fast enough to have skipped the clips.

Exclusion rule candidates

The questionnaire README defines the analysis sample as “completed and passed the checks”, so exclusion was planned, just not pinned to specific rules. The consequences of several rules:

[[T_EXCL]]

My recommendation (confirmed by you): primary sample = strict AT1 + AV1 ([[N_BOTH]] people), because these two checks were built into the questionnaire at design time; “all 300” and “additionally dropping the three-page straightliners” as sensitivity analyses. Do not exclude on BEL1 (belief): it is measured after the clips and after every outcome, and “not believing” may be exactly the reaction of someone whose condition description clashed with what the clips made them feel — excluding on it would remove part of the condition effect and create selection bias. BEL1 is better used as a moderator or in a believers-only sensitivity analysis.

Covariate balance

Participants were randomised, so background variables should be similar in expectation. With finite samples, chance imbalance always occurs. The two clearest cases here: age — HA1 averages 49.7 years, H2 only 35.4; positive attitude to AI — 4.48 in A, 4.8–5.2 in every other condition. The second matters most: GAAIS positive attitude correlates r = .46 with the OH composite, so if A participants disliked AI to begin with, part of A's low scores could be “different people” rather than “different condition”. The remedy is not to drop people but to add these pre-measure variables as covariates in a secondary model and compare conclusions before and after adjustment.

Phase 6 · Primary framework

Primary framework: the linear mixed model

The model

DV ~ condition + clip + position + (1 | participant)

In plain words: each person gives one score per clip. Score = the condition's average level + how easy or hard this clip is + which position it was shown in + this person's own baseline + leftover random error. (1 | participant) is “each person's own baseline”; it acknowledges that a person's three scores are correlated. What it estimates: each condition's expected score “for the same clip in the same position”; the differences between conditions are the effects we want.

Assumption checks

[[T_MODELS]]
  • ICC (intraclass correlation) is .40–.78: more than half of the variation in scores comes from “different people”, not “different clips”. This is the evidence that a mixed model is required, and it explains why condition effects that look obvious in the 900-row plots are not easy to detect.
  • Residual skew −0.35 to −1.06: left-skewed (high scores piled at the top), moderate. The mixed model is not very sensitive to this, but ordinal models and the bootstrap are used to double-check.
  • Unequal variances: the most important assumption problem found in this round. Between-person spread in A is 1.3–1.5 times that in H/HA (table below, Levene p ≤ .014). The primary model assumes one variance for everyone, so for any contrast involving A the model's standard error may be too small and its p-value optimistic. Remedy: re-check with methods that do not assume equal variances (Welch t, Mann–Whitney, participant bootstrap), see section 13.
[[T_LEV]]
How to read the mixed-model output (OH composite as the example)

Intercept 5.47: the expected score of the reference cell (H1, clip REL, position 1). condition[A] −0.40, SE 0.22, z −1.81, p = .071: A is 0.40 points below H1; SE is the uncertainty of that estimate; z = estimate ÷ SE; p is “the probability of a z this large if the true difference were zero”. clip[ADV] +0.36, p < .001: the advice clip scores 0.36 above the chat clip, for everyone. Group Var: the variance of the between-person baselines. The paper should report: estimate, SE or CI, test statistic, p, and the variance components or ICC.

Phase 7–8 · Pairwise comparisons

All pairwise comparisons between conditions

Scope: 11 outcomes × all 21 pairs of the 7 conditions (CR3 has only 6 conditions, 15 pairs). Differences come from the primary model (clip level) or an ordinary linear model (participant level); d is standardised on the pooled SD of participant-level means. Each outcome's 21 pairs form one “statistical family”; Holm and BH corrections are applied within the family. Unadjusted raw p first, to see the complete map; but remember: with 225 comparisons and no effects at all, about 11 would still show raw p < .05.

Phase 9 · Pattern mapping

Pattern mapping

  • Unique condition: of the 84 pairwise comparisons on the four primary outcomes, 10 have raw p < .05, and every one is “some human cell > A”, in the same direction. Of the 60 comparisons among the six human cells, zero are significant. This is neither a gradient nor a cluster structure: A stands alone.
  • No profile structure: no pair within H1/H2/H3 or within HA1/HA2/HA3 is significant, and no 1 > 2 > 3 ordering is visible.
  • CR3 is a second pattern: H cells above HA cells (4 of the 9 H–HA pairs significant, no differences within H or within HA), evidence that the “AI assistance” sentence was taken in.
  • Reading the factor structure: “human vs no human” has a main effect (AU1, CR1, weaker on OH); “H vs HA” has none; “profile” has none, and there are no interactions.
  • One cell to watch: HA1 is the highest on many items (HA1 > HA3 on HM is significant). HA1 is also the oldest and most AI-positive group. One or two significant pairs among 21 cells are expected by chance; before treating it as a finding, check whether it survives covariate adjustment (section 13: the C3 difference shrinks after adjustment, consistent with this).

Do not chain p-values: “H3 > A is significant (p < .001) and HA3 > A is not (p = .06)” does not imply “H3 differs from HA3”. The direct comparison of H3 and HA3 gives p = .80, a difference of 0.02 points. One comparison crossing the line and another not is usually a matter of precision (here HA3 − A is 0.55, H3 − A is 1.09, with largely overlapping intervals). To say two effects differ, test the interaction or the “contrast of contrasts”.

Phase 11 · Contrasts

Theory-driven contrasts

Research rationale and statistical definition of each contrast: C1 the mean of the six human-involved cells vs A, answering “does telling people a human is involved change perception”; C2 the mean of the three H cells vs the three HA cells, answering “does the AI-assistance sentence itself do anything”; C3 the mean of H2, H3, HA2, HA3 vs the mean of H1, HA1, answering “does disclosing any disability change perception” — pooling presumes the two disabilities act alike, so C4 intellectual vs mobility is tested alongside; C5a/C5b split C3. All contrasts are equal-weight means of cells, so slightly unequal cell sizes do not affect them.

[[T_CON]]
  • C1 human vs AI-only: AU1 genuineness differs by 0.81 points, d = .62 (medium to large), p = .001, Holm .002; CR1 warmth by 0.50, d = .52, p = .004; the OH composite by 0.40, d = .42, p = .020 (Holm .040, BH .080); CR2 competence by 0.22, p = .135. Reading: being told “controlled entirely by AI” makes the interaction feel more like a program running and the controller less warm, but leaves competence untouched.
  • C2 H vs HA: d on the four primary outcomes lies between −.17 and +.13, and the confidence intervals roughly exclude |d| > .35. This is not “failed to detect” but “if there is a difference, it is small”. CR3 is the exception (H higher by 0.40, p = .002), and it is supposed to differ.
  • C3 disability disclosed vs no mention: all negative (slightly lower after disclosure) but OH −0.09, AU1 −0.29 (d = −.24, p = .112), CR1 −0.21 (d = −.25, p = .114), CR2 −0.11, none significant. C4 intellectual vs mobility gives d ≤ .17 on every outcome, so pooling is justified and the two disabilities were not treated differently. C5a intellectual vs no mention comes closest on AU1 (−0.39, p = .061).
  • Participant level: PE and HM show no significant contrast; HM's C3 −0.40 (p = .076) and C5b −0.45 (p = .079) are the only hints.
Phase 12 · Interactions and simple effects

Interactions and simple effects

Interactions are tested by likelihood-ratio tests (LRT): the model with the interaction is compared with the model without it, asking whether the likelihood improves by more than chance. The table shows p-values.

[[T_INT]]
  • Clip × control source: OH composite p = .13, the others larger. The descriptive impression that “A loses most in REL” did not pass the test; all that can be said is that A's penalty varies in size across clips but not in direction.
  • Two hints on CR2: clip × human/AI-only p = .051; the simple effects below show the AI rated less competent only in the chat clip (REL; d = .44, p = .078) and not at all in the advice and collaboration clips. Position × condition p = .012 has no theoretical reading; one .012 among 8 outcomes on a 12-df test is unremarkable, so it is logged as an exploratory lead.
  • Control source × profile: all p ≥ .18. The (tiny) disclosure effect does not differ between H and HA.

Simple effects within each clip (exploratory)

[[T_SIMPLE]]

Simple effects are row-level Welch t-tests used only to see the shape of the interaction, not as standalone conclusions; the clips differ in length (70/105/115 s), which is confounded with task type, a design limitation.

RQ4 · Attribution of responsibility and credit

Attribution of responsibility and credit

Method: whether an actor was ranked first is treated as a binary outcome and modelled by GEE logistic regression (participant clusters, clip as covariate), giving the odds ratio (OR) between conditions. OR = 1 means no difference; 0.5 means “the odds of being ranked first are halved”. The responsibility-vs-credit asymmetry is compared at the participant level (share of a person's three clips in which the actor was ranked first, responsibility minus credit) with the Wilcoxon signed-rank test.

[[T_RANKS]]
  • HA vs H: adding “AI assistance” lowers the share ranking the human operator as most responsible from 72% to 55% (OR .47, p = .001) and as most creditable from 71% to 51% (OR .42, p < .001). The share moved away goes mainly to the AI (ranked first in about 15% of HA rows) and to the person in the video.
  • Correction for the different option sets (added after the cross-review): H offers 3 rows and HA 4, so the odds of being ranked first fall mechanically when an option is added. On a common anchor — whether the operator is ranked ahead of both “OriHime's provider” and “the person in the video” — responsibility is H 72.3% vs HA 60.7% (OR .59, p = .022) and credit 71.2% vs 60.2% (OR .61, p = .032). The shift is not purely mechanical, but “odds halved” mixes real reallocation with the added option; the paper uses the common-anchor numbers.
  • A vs HA: with no human, the share ranking the AI first jumps from 15% to 57% (responsibility) and 60% (credit), OR ≈ 7.8. On the common anchor the jump largely disappears: the AI is ranked ahead of the provider and the interlocutor in HA 52% vs A 57% (responsibility, OR 1.24, p = .47) and 49% vs 60% (credit, OR 1.60, p = .14). The AI's standing relative to the provider and the interlocutor is similar in both groups; the 7.8 mainly reflects that the person is no longer on the list.
  • Disability disclosure: OR .89 (responsibility) and .80 (credit), both non-significant: disclosing the operator's disability did not make participants assign the operator more or less responsibility.
  • Responsibility > credit asymmetry: only in HA (the operator is ranked first 4.8 points more often for responsibility than for credit, p = .024); not in H, and not for the AI in A. A very small effect, “suggestive”.
[[T_ANCHOR]]

Participant-level outcomes and the credibility check

[[T_PL]]

BEL1 does not differ across the 7 conditions (omnibus p = .45; C1 p = .30), so the three descriptions were believed to the same degree — the credibility check passes (it asks about the whole description, not the disability sentence, so it cannot show that the disability label was believed). It does not show that the manipulation worked; the evidence for that is CR3: people told that AI assisted rated the operator as less in control (H 6.14 vs HA 5.73, d = .40, p = .002). The AI-only condition has no direct check; the only indirect evidence is that A participants assign responsibility and credit to the AI.

Phase 13 · Robustness and sensitivity

Robustness and sensitivity analyses

Each method listed has its own rationale: alternative sample definitions (does the exclusion rule drive the conclusion); pre-measure covariates (GAAIS, NARS, SCM warmth, age — because these are imbalanced by chance after randomisation and correlate .3–.5 with the outcomes); Welch t and Mann–Whitney on participant means (no equal-variance or normality assumption); participant bootstrap (added after Levene's test found larger variance in A, a data-informed robustness check); ordinal GEE (1–7 as ordered categories, answering the ceiling effect; single items only). Cells show the difference (or odds ratio) and p.

[[T_ROB]] [[T_ORD]]
  • The C1 effect on AU1 holds under every method (largest p .023): the only perception effect in this round that is significant under every specification.
  • The C1 effect on CR1 is significant under the model, covariate adjustment, bootstrap and ordinal GEE, and just over the line under Welch t (.052) and Mann–Whitney (.092): the difference is real but the precision limited.
  • The C1 effect on the OH composite is significant only under the equal-variance model methods (.019–.039); the variance-robust methods give p = .055–.069. It is driven by OH2 usefulness (ordinal GEE p = .031) and OH3 willingness; OH1 trustworthiness itself is not significant (p = .10–.17). So “AI control lowers trust” is not supported; “lowers usefulness and willingness” is.
  • C3 disclosure: negative under every method, p ≈ .06–.07 under the bootstrap and Welch, but after adding covariates the AU1 difference shrinks from −0.29 to −0.10 (p = .57). Part of this hint is the no-mention group happening to be older and more AI-positive; it cannot be reported as a finding, only as a direction worth replicating.
  • The exclusion rule (272 vs 300 vs 257) changes no conclusion.
Phase 14 · Exploratory analyses E1–E8

Exploratory analyses E1–E8

Bookkeeping first. The table shows how many tests this round ran; in E5 “significant” means “equivalent”, and E7's mean-rank comparisons do not count as findings (see below), so the tests that really need a false-positive check are the few in E1, E2, E6 and E8.

[[T_E_BOOK]]

E1 · Attitude × control source moderation (post hoc)

Why it is legitimate: attitudes were measured before the manipulation, so they are valid moderators; theoretically “controlled entirely by AI” is bad news for AI sceptics and not for AI enthusiasts. Statistics: an LMM with a human × attitude interaction tested by LRT; then the human − AI-only difference at 1 SD below the mean attitude, at the mean, and 1 SD above.

[[T_E1]]
  • The GAAIS positive attitude × human/AI-only interaction is significant on OH (p = .019) and CR2 (p = .003), with AU1 (.083) and CR1 (.093) in the same direction. In the three-level test HA's slope (.30) is almost identical to H's (.26), while A's is .50: AI assistance does not trigger attitude-dependence, only “entirely AI” does.
  • For participants 1 SD below the mean attitude, the AI-only penalty is OH 0.44, AU1 0.91, CR1 0.62, CR2 0.42 points, all p ≤ .015; for participants 1 SD above, the differences lie between −0.36 and +0.20, none significant. Note: the reversed difference “AI-positive people rate the AI-controlled OriHime higher” is not itself significant and must not be written that way.
  • This also explains the unequal variances found in section 6: A's larger spread arises because attitude matters more in A.
  • Status: post hoc (it was seen once in the 09-02 report); 3 of 14 tests significant; reportable only as an exploratory finding in need of replication.

E2 · BEL1 sensitivity analysis

Why it is legitimate: if the condition effect really comes from the description, it should hold among people who believed the description. Limitation: BEL1 is measured after the manipulation and may itself be a reaction to the condition.

[[T_E2B]]
  • Believers only (BEL1 ≥ 5, 206 people): C1 on OH (p = .025), AU1 (.001) and CR1 (.006) is almost identical to the primary sample; C3 remains non-significant. The conclusion is insensitive to belief.
  • Among believers AU1's H vs HA comes out at +0.39 (p = .037): one of 28 tests, .38 in the primary sample, and in the opposite direction to the “AI assistance penalises” intuition; logged, not reported.
  • The human × BEL1 interaction is negative on all four outcomes (CR1 p = .036, CR2 p = .009), literally “disbelievers are penalised more”. But only 3 people in A disbelieved the description, so the interaction rests on 3 people and is uninterpretable.
[[T_E2I]]

E3 · OH single items

[[T_E3]]

Under the participant bootstrap only OH2 usefulness is significant (p = .036); OH3 willingness .085, OH1 trustworthiness .146. Consistent with the ordinal GEE in section 13. The wording should be “AI-only control lowered the rated usefulness of OriHime; no detectable difference in trustworthiness”.

E4 · Moderators of the disability disclosure

Why it is legitimate: a disclosure effect might appear only in specific tasks or people; contact with people with disabilities (the contact hypothesis) and stereotype content (SCM) are the most direct theoretical moderators, both measured before the manipulation.

[[T_E4]]
  • 20 tests, 0 significant. Disclosure × clip comes close on CR1 (.068) and CR2 (.080); simple effects show disclosure lowering warmth by 0.33 in the collaboration clip (p = .014) and not in the advice clip, but the overall interaction is not significant, so “only in the collaboration task” cannot be claimed.
  • Disclosure × contact frequency: all p ≥ .36; disclosure × SCM warmth on AU1: people with colder stereotypes drop 0.43 after disclosure (p = .054), warmer ones −0.08, a sensible direction but the interaction itself is p = .27.
  • Conclusion: the disclosure has no main effect in these data and no detectable conditional effect either.

E5 · Equivalence tests (TOST)

Why it is legitimate: “p > .05” is not “the same”. TOST (two one-sided tests) asks the reverse question: is the difference small enough to fall inside a pre-specified “negligible” interval? Bounds are ±0.35 SD (the smallest effect this sample detects reliably) and ±0.50 SD (a medium effect). TOST p < .05 means “equivalence can be claimed”.

[[T_E5]]
  • H vs HA: passes the ±0.35 SD equivalence test on OH, AU1, CR1 and HM, and ±0.50 SD on every outcome; CR3 is not equivalent (it is supposed to differ). The ±0.35 bound comes from this design's detectable effect, not a domain-defined smallest important difference; “the difference is bounded within ±0.35 SD” is safer wording than “statistically equivalent”.
  • Disability disclosed vs no mention: OH is equivalent within ±0.35; AU1, CR1 and HM have 90% CI lower bounds down to −0.47 and can only be called equivalent within ±0.50. Wording: “if an effect exists, it is smaller than medium (|d| < 0.5)”.
  • Intellectual vs mobility: equivalent within ±0.35 on everything except AU1.

E6 · Two interaction leads on CR2

The REL-specific competence penalty: tested with the one-degree-of-freedom contrast “human × is this the chat clip”, interaction = 0.33, p = .037; but within the chat clip the ordinal model's human vs AI-only odds ratio is 1.78, p = .108. That is, the difference “AI looks less competent in the chat and not in the task clips” is barely significant, while the penalty within the chat clip is not. Status: post hoc lead.

[[T_E6A]]

Position × condition (round-1 p = .012): decomposed, human × position p = .51, control source × position p = .62, profile × position p = .061. The original .012 comes from irregular ups and downs in individual cells (H1 at position 3: 6.13, H3 at position 3: 5.52) and matches no theoretical line. Dropped.

[[T_E6B]]

E7 · Ranking models

Why it is legitimate: round 1 used only “who is first” and discarded second and third places. The Plackett–Luce model treats a ranking as successive choices and estimates each actor's “worth” (summing to 1 within a condition).

[[T_E7C]] [[T_E7D]]

In HA, when the operator is ranked first, second place goes to the AI 64–67% of the time, to the provider 21–25%, and to the person in the video only 11–12%: the AI is already seen as the next responsible party.

[[T_E7A]]

The responsibility > credit asymmetry points the same way in all three clips (3–6 points each), is not significant in any single clip, and the pooled GEE odds ratio is 1.21 (p = .032) with no clip interaction (p = .89). A stable but very small effect.

E7b's “mean rank” comparisons (H vs HA, HA vs A, all p < .001) cannot count as findings: H offers 3 options and HA 4, so mean ranks change mechanically with the number of options. Removed from the bookkeeping table, kept in the CSV for verification.

“Significant” only when compared within HA (−0.69, p = .022), −0.11 within H (p = .69); after covariate adjustment it shrinks to −0.21 (p = .26). Trial storyline and paper drafts

Storyline summary

  1. Setup. The clips are identical for everyone; the only variable is one page of text: who controls, and who the operator is. Condition differences can only come from prior attribution.
  2. What human presence changes. People told “controlled entirely by AI” found the interaction less genuine (d .62, holds under every method), the controller less warm (d .52, suggestive), and OriHime less useful and less worth taking part in (suggestive); competence and trustworthiness unchanged.
  3. What does not change. The difference between human and human-with-AI operation is bounded within ±0.35 SD (OH, genuineness, warmth, hedonic motivation) or ±0.50 SD (all); explicit disability disclosure has no detectable average effect, point estimates small and negative, effects beyond half an SD excluded, no moderator found; but “AI assistance” did reduce perceived operator control (d .40), so the text was taken in.
  4. The cost lands on attribution. AI assistance lowers the odds that the operator is ranked first (raw OR .47 / .42, common-anchor OR .59 / .61); the lost share goes almost entirely to the AI (Plackett–Luce .68 → .45, AI .28); with no human the AI takes .55; in HA the operator is blamed slightly more than credited.
  5. Coda (exploratory). The AI-only penalty is concentrated among participants less positive about AI; HA's slope equals H's, so only the absence of a human triggers attitude-dependence.

Conclusions the data do not support

  • “AI-controlled robots are trusted less” — the trustworthiness item shows no difference.
  • “Disclosing a disability changes how the operator is judged” — not detected in this material; effects beyond half an SD are excluded but small-to-moderate negative effects are not; the two disabilities do not differ.
  • “AI assistance undermines the operator's standing” — perception is equivalent; only attribution shifts.
  • “The disability effect appears in particular tasks or people” — all 20 moderator tests non-significant.
  • “People who like AI prefer AI control” — the reversed difference at +1 SD is not significant.

Appendix A · Supplementary sweep of the disability factor

Supplementary sweep of the disability factor

Corrections after the cross-review

On 2026-09-04 an independent cross-review (analysis/study1-20260902-cross-review/workbook_cross_review.html) recomputed every number in this appendix — all matched — but found the narration too strong. The appendix has been revised accordingly; corrected points are marked “cross-review” in each subsection. Key points: Bayes factors are now computed on participant units; “was read” and “was believed” are stated separately; D1 and D2 cannot exclude the label being overridden within the first clip; the conclusion is limited to “the incremental effect of an explicit one-sentence disclosure”; the stereotype pre-measure, the equal-training assurance and the competent clips all weaken a label and count as boundary conditions.

Answers to the four questions

Q1Is there really nothing left to find? In these data and this material, no significant effect can be found. Eight further analyses targeted the disability factor: 31 tests, 3 with p < .05 against 1.6 expected by chance, and none of the 3 is “disclosure has an effect”. Bayes factors on participant units: OH 11.7, competence 9.2, performance expectancy 9.6, operator control 7.6 favour the null; genuineness 3.5 and warmth 3.2 are only weak-to-moderate; hedonic motivation 2.8. All four primary point estimates are negative, genuineness and warmth d ≈ −.24 with 95% intervals reaching −.5 and participant-level p .06–.09. The correct statement: not detected, large effects excluded, small-to-moderate negative effects not excluded. (Corrected after the 2026-09-04 cross-review: the earlier “20 to 1 for the null” used the wrong information unit.)

Q2What would more participants do to the p-values? If the effect size stays the same, z grows with the square root of the sample size. Genuineness and warmth currently have d ≈ −.24, p .11: doubling the human cells would bring p to about .025; the smallest effect this design detects reliably for C3 is d = .39, power at d = .24 is only 41%, and 80% power would need about 610 people against the current 236. Three reasons not to do it: after adding pre-measure covariates the estimates shrink to −.10 and −.18, so part of the −.24 may come from the no-mention group happening to be older (46.1 vs 38.8 years) and more AI-positive — though that is a post hoc sensitivity analysis and cannot adjudicate alone; deciding to add people after seeing p = .11 is “optional stopping”, which inflates the false-positive rate and must be declared; and even if significant, 0.3 points on a 7-point scale cannot carry a story. If it must be tested, open a preregistered replication cell with a stronger disclosure and a fixed sample size and analysis.

Q3Could the clips be non-neutral? The suspicion is reasonable: competence ratings in the human conditions average 5.86 and 73% are ≥ 6; participants watched a fluent, competent operator, which leaves the “intellectual disability” label little room to act. Two more features of the material weaken the label: nine stereotype items about people with disabilities were answered before the disclosure, and the disclosure sentence was followed immediately by the assurance that all operators complete the same training and meet the same standard. The data can say only so much: there is evidence the sentence was read (the disclosure cells dwelt 5.5 s longer on the page, p .023, consistent with reading one sentence), but whether it was remembered or believed was not measured directly — BEL1 asks about the whole description; after the first clip there is no difference between disclosure and no mention (D2), but with no pre-clip baseline the label could have acted and been overridden by behavioural evidence within the first clip, so “the clip overrides the label” is neither supported nor excluded. The default impression the clips give can be tested directly in Study 2: a new sample with no description answers “does this person have a disability”; if most say “no” or “can't tell”, the clips carry a “no disability” signal that conflicts with the H2/H3 descriptions — but that cannot retroactively show that Study 1's participants read or believed the sentence.

Q4Can disability still open the story? Yes, but only as a “bounded null” story: open with OriHime being operated by people with disabilities, ask “does knowing the operator has a disability change how the interaction is judged”, and answer “not detectably in this material: effects beyond half an SD are excluded, participant-unit Bayes factors of 3–12 favour the null, evaluations and attribution show no detectable change, and the only thing that changed evaluations was whether a human was present at all”. The price is answering three objections head-on: the one-sentence disclosure is weak, the competence assurance that follows it neutralises the stereotype, and whether the label was believed was not measured. Available answers are the dwell time, Study 2's default impression and the TOST bounds, with visible or repeated disclosure and a disability-specific recall item listed as future work. The data support neither “disclosure changed evaluations” nor the general claim “disability status itself has no effect”.

Checklist of tests

Already tested before this sweep: the pooled contrast C3 and its parts C4, C5a, C5b (all outcomes); control source × profile interaction; profile × clip; three moderators (contact frequency, SCM warmth, SCM competence); attribution rankings; PE, HM, BEL1; equivalence tests. None significant. The eight new analyses below add 31 tests, 3 with p < .05 against about 1.6 expected.

[[T_D_BOOK]]

D1 · Disclosure effect × presentation position

Why it is legitimate: if fluent, competent behaviour in the clips overrides the “has a disability” label, the disclosure effect should be largest on the first clip and fade afterwards. Result: after the first clip the disclosure effect is near zero (differences of −0.03 to −0.15 on the five outcomes); on the third clip a consistent −0.26 to −0.37 appears (p .03–.056), with significant interactions on OH (p = .026) and CR2 (p = .033).

[[T_D1]] [[T_D1M]]

Reading: the no-mention group's ratings rise with position (the general position effect), the disclosure group's do not. This is not “the label washed out by the clips”; it looks more like “the label's influence accumulating”, or simply noise: it is post hoc, 2 of 5 interactions are significant, and the outcomes are correlated, so it is a lead, not a finding. It cannot refute “fast override”: the first measurement comes after 70–115 s of video, there is no post-disclosure, pre-clip baseline, and the label may have acted and been overridden within the first clip (pointed out by the 2026-09-04 cross-review).

D2 · First clip only

Right after reading the description and watching one clip, disclosure and no-mention ratings are almost identical (d between −.04 and −.10, p ≥ .41). That is, no detectable difference after the first clip; with no pre-clip measurement, this does not mean the label never acted immediately.

[[T_D2]]

D3 · The warmth-minus-competence stereotype pattern

The stereotype content model predicts a paternalistic “high warmth, low competence” pattern for disability, i.e. a wider warmth-minus-competence gap after disclosure. The gap actually narrows slightly (−0.10, p = .18; intellectual disability −0.13, p = .076): no paternalistic pattern.

D4 · Attribution within each control source

[[T_D4]]

All 14 tests non-significant. The closest: within HA, after disclosure the share ranking “the person in the video” first for credit rises from 16% to 28% (OR 1.87, p = .13), and in HA3 the operator is ranked first for credit only 45% of the time. Interesting direction, insufficient evidence.

D5 · Belief by profile

The two intellectual-disability cells average BEL1 4.96 against 5.27 in the other human cells (p = .17); H2 is lowest at 4.85 with 23% disbelievers against 15% in H1. Directionally, “a human operator with an intellectual disability” is the hardest of the six descriptions to believe, but the difference is not significant.

D6 · Dwell time on the disclosure page

Participants told of a disability spent a median 45.7 s on the description page against 40.2 s for no mention (Mann–Whitney p = .023; medians 57 and 52 s in HA2 and HA3). There is evidence the extra sentence was read: 5.5 s matches the reading time of a sentence of a dozen words. Whether it was remembered or believed was not measured directly; BEL1 asks about the whole description. Dwell time does not moderate the disclosure effect (p ≥ .49). This can go in the paper to answer “was it even read”, not “was it believed”.

D7 · Bayes factors

[[T_D7]]

BF01 is the evidence for “no disclosure effect” relative to “some effect”. D8 · Sample-size projections

How p changes with sample size: if the effect size stays the same, z scales with √(multiplier). AU1 and CR1 currently have d ≈ −.24: adding as many people again to the human cells (236 → 472) would bring p to about .025, and 610 people would give 80% power to detect d = .24. Three things make this route unattractive: after covariate adjustment the estimates shrink to −.10 and −.18, so part of the −.24 is the no-mention group being older and more AI-positive by chance; deciding to add people after seeing p = .11 and testing again is “optional stopping”, which inflates the false-positive rate, must be declared in the paper and will be questioned by reviewers; and even if significant, a 0.3-point difference on a 7-point scale is small and cannot carry the story. If more data are collected, the right way is a separate preregistered replication cell with a stronger disclosure (repeated, visible cues), not topping up the current cells.

Neutrality of the clips

  • The operator in the clips performs fluently; competence ratings in the human conditions average 5.86 with 72% of ratings ≥ 6. Participants saw competent performance, which does compress the room for an “intellectual disability” label to act. But this is part of the design: every condition watches the same clips, which is what isolates the effect as prior attribution.
  • What the data can say: there is evidence the label was read (D6), but remembering and believing were not measured directly; no difference after the first clip (D2), but with no pre-clip baseline a fast override cannot be excluded (D1). So “the clip overrides the label” is neither directly supported nor excluded in these data; the more accurate statement is “given observed competent performance, a one-off textual disclosure does not change evaluations”.
  • The data that can test the clips' neutrality directly are Study 2's. Study 2 asks a new sample, with no description, “who is controlling” and “does the person have a disability”. If most answer “no disability” or “can't tell”, the clips carry a “no disability” signal that conflicts with the H2/H3 descriptions, the strongest available explanation of this study's null; if answers are spread out, the clips are neutral and the null is more likely genuine. This step is far cheaper than adding participants.

Feasibility of opening the story with disability

Yes, but only as a bounded story: open with OriHime being operated by people with disabilities, ask “does knowing the operator has a disability change how people judge the interaction”, and answer “not detectably in this material: there is evidence the label was read, evaluations and attribution show no detectable change (effects beyond half an SD excluded, participant-unit BF01 3–12), and the only thing that changed evaluations was whether a human was present”. This is a conclusion with positive implications for inclusive remote work. Its price is facing the objection that a one-sentence disclosure is too weak — answered with D6, Study 2 and the TOST bounds — while acknowledging that BEL1 is not disability-specific and that the “same training” assurance in the material weakens the label, and listing visible or repeated disclosure and a disability-specific recall item as future work. What the data do not support is any claim that “disclosing a disability changed evaluations”.

Statistical concepts

Statistical concepts

Mean and standard deviation SD

Plainly: the mean is the average level; the SD is how spread out people are. An SD of 1.0 on a 7-point scale means most people fall within 1 point of the mean; an SD of 1.8 (A's AU1) means large disagreement. Why now: when comparing two groups the difference has to be read against the spread — a 0.3-point difference is medium when the SD is 1.0 and almost invisible when it is 1.8. Common mistake: reading means without the SD and n.

95% confidence interval CI

Plainly: the sample mean is one estimate of the “true mean”; the CI gives a range showing how precise that estimate is. More people and a smaller SD give a narrower interval. Formal definition: if the same study were repeated many times and an interval computed each time, about 95% of those intervals would contain the true value. How to read it: A's OH interval [4.99, 5.68] is twice as wide as H's [5.44, 5.78] because A has only 44 people. Common mistakes: overlapping intervals do not mean “no significant difference” (a slight overlap can still be significant); and one cannot say “there is a 95% probability the true value is in this interval”.

Cronbach's α (internal consistency)

Plainly: if several items measure the same thing, their answers should rise and fall together; α measures that agreement (0–1). Why now: to decide whether OH1–3 may be averaged into one OH score. Rules of thumb: ≥ .70 acceptable, ≥ .80 good; but a very high α may only mean the items repeat each other (PE's .96 has that flavour), and α does not prove the items measure a single dimension. Common mistake: treating a low α as a broken scale — with few items α is naturally lower.

Floor and ceiling effects

Plainly: when many people give the top score (7), there is no room to go higher. Consequence: real differences are compressed, the distribution skews left, and means and SDs describe it poorly. What to do: report the share choosing 7, and use ordinal models for robustness (1–7 as ordered categories rather than equally spaced numbers).

Unit of analysis, repeated measures and nesting

Plainly: one person answered three clips, and the three answers resemble each other because they come from the same person. Treating them as three independent people triples the sample size. The mixed model gives each person their own baseline (a random intercept) and estimates condition effects on top of it. That is what the (1 | participant) term means.

Randomisation and covariate balance

Plainly: random assignment makes groups the same “on average”, but any one sample can be imbalanced by chance (age and GAAIS here). Handling: no deletions, no regrouping; add the pre-measures as covariates in a secondary model and see whether conclusions change. Note: only variables measured before the manipulation may be used this way; BEL1, measured after, may not.

p-values and “significance”

Plainly: the p-value answers “if the true difference were zero, how likely is a difference at least this large by luck alone”. p = .004 means such luck occurs about four times in a thousand. α = .05 is a convention, not a law of nature: p = .051 and p = .049 provide almost the same evidence. It does not answer: how large the difference is (see d) or “the probability the hypothesis is true”. Common mistake: reading p > .05 as “no effect” — it only means “these data cannot separate it from zero”.

Effect size Cohen's d

Plainly: how many standard deviations apart two group means are. 0.2 small, 0.5 medium, 0.8 large is only a rough yardstick. The C1 effect on AU1, d = .62: a typical A participant is 0.6 SD below a typical human-condition participant, about 0.8 points on the 1–7 scale. The confidence interval of d, [.26, 1.02], shows how imprecise the estimate is — A has only 36 people, so the interval is wide.

ICC and the likelihood-ratio test LRT

ICC: the share of total variance that lies “between people”. An ICC of .66 means knowing who answered explains two thirds of the differences in scores. LRT: compares the log-likelihoods of two nested models (one with an extra set of parameters); twice the difference approximately follows a chi-square distribution with degrees of freedom equal to the number of extra parameters. It answers “is this set of parameters useful as a whole”, e.g. “does the condition factor have any effect”, “does the interaction have any effect”.

Contrasts and interactions

Contrast: one number formed by adding and subtracting cell means with weights, e.g. C1 = mean of the six human cells − A. Its test asks “is this number zero”. Contrasts are more powerful than pairwise comparisons because they pool related cells. Interaction: whether one factor's effect changes with another, e.g. “does A's penalty differ across the three clips”. Simple effect: the interaction taken apart, the effect within each clip. Only when the interaction test is significant may simple effects be called “different”.

Unequal variances, the bootstrap and odds ratios

Unequal variances: groups differ in spread. A's participants disagree more; a standard model treats everyone as equally spread and understates the uncertainty of A's mean. Bootstrap: resample participants with replacement within each condition thousands of times, recompute the contrast each time, and look at its distribution — no equal-variance or normality assumption. Odds ratio OR: the effect size of logistic regression, the ratio of the odds of an event between two groups; OR .47 means the odds of the human operator being ranked first in HA are 47% of those in H.

Manipulation checks, credibility checks, and HARKing

Credibility check (BEL1): did participants believe the description — it passes when belief is equal across conditions. Manipulation check: did the description change the perception it was meant to change — here carried by CR3 (HA sees the operator as less in control). Directional hypothesis: predicts the direction of a difference and decides between one- and two-tailed tests; we use two-tailed throughout. Preregistration: publicly registering hypotheses and analyses before seeing the data; without it, the substitute is what this workbook does — writing down the primary sample, outcomes, model and contrasts before any p-value is seen, and labelling everything later as exploratory. HARKing: presenting hypotheses formed after seeing the results as if they had existed beforehand; the only difference from exploratory analysis is honest labelling.

Moderation and simple slopes

Plainly: “does the effect differ between people”. A product term “human × attitude” is added to the model; its coefficient is the difference between the two groups' slopes, and significance means the slopes differ. Simple slopes / conditional effects: the group difference at a given attitude value (e.g. 1 SD below and above the mean). Common mistake: concluding from a significant interaction that “AI-positive people prefer AI” — that needs the difference at +1 SD itself to be significant, and here it is not.

Post-treatment variables are not ordinary moderators

BEL1 is measured after the manipulation and may be a result of the condition: someone in A who found the interaction genuine may for that very reason disbelieve that “the AI is in control”. Grouping on it uses part of the outcome as a grouping variable. So it can only serve sensitivity analyses (does the conclusion hold among believers), not causal claims like “the effect is concentrated among believers”.

Equivalence tests TOST

Ordinary tests ask “is the difference zero” and cannot “prove sameness”. TOST first fixes a bound for “small enough not to matter” (here ±0.35 or ±0.50 SD), then runs two one-sided tests: is the difference significantly above the lower bound and significantly below the upper one. If both are significant, equivalence can be claimed — equivalently, the 90% confidence interval lies entirely inside the bounds. The bounds must be set before looking at the data; we used the smallest detectable effect from the sensitivity analysis.

Ranking data: why mean ranks mislead, and what Plackett–Luce computes

H has 3 options and HA 4: the same actor's mean rank is naturally larger in HA, so comparing mean ranks across conditions is invalid. Plackett–Luce treats a ranking as “pick the first from all actors, then the second from the rest…” and estimates each actor's “worth”, summing to 1 within a condition, so 3-option and 4-option conditions can be compared on the same scale.

Bookkeeping of exploratory tests

The exploration phase ran 113 tests. At a 5% false-positive rate, about 6 would show p < .05 even with no effects at all. So an exploratory finding is judged not only by its p but by whether the direction is consistent across related outcomes (E1 yes), whether one cell drives it (E8 yes, dropped), and whether it survives a change of method (E6's position interaction did not, dropped). All tests are kept in CSV and belong in the paper's supplement.

How large an effect this sample can see (sensitivity)

Plainly: sample size decides how small a difference can be found reliably. In Cohen's d (difference divided by SD; 0.2 small, 0.5 medium, 0.8 large), with 80% power and α = .05: “human vs AI-only” (primary sample 236 vs 36) detects d ≥ 0.50, H vs HA (118 vs 118) detects d ≥ 0.36, disability disclosed vs no mention (159 vs 77) detects d ≥ 0.39, two profile levels (about 78 vs 80) detect d ≥ 0.44, and one condition vs one condition (43 vs 43) needs d ≥ 0.60. So among the 21 pairwise comparisons many real small differences will be “non-significant” — non-significant is not no effect, the keynote of the interpretation.
